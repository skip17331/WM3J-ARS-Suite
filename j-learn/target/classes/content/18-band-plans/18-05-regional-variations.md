# Regional Variations

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

The amateur band allocations in the United States (FCC 47 CFR §97) are not the same as in Europe, in Japan, or in Australia. Three reasons:

1. **The world is divided into three ITU regions** for radio regulation, with each region having its own frequency allocation table.
2. **Each country's national regulator** sets specific privileges within ITU-allocated bands, sometimes more restrictively than the ITU baseline.
3. **Voluntary band plans** (CW vs. SSB vs. digital sub-bands) are coordinated regionally by IARU bodies — IARU Region 1, IARU Region 2, IARU Region 3 — and differ slightly between regions.

When you work DX, the station on the other end may have different sub-band privileges than you. Knowing this prevents calling someone in a "wrong" segment and helps you understand why DX-peditions operate split.

## The three ITU regions

The ITU (International Telecommunication Union) divides the world into three regions for purposes of frequency allocation:

| Region | Geography |
|--------|-----------|
| Region 1 | Europe, Africa, the Middle East west of the Persian Gulf, and the former Soviet Union |
| Region 2 | The Americas — North, Central, and South America, plus Greenland and the Pacific east of 160° E |
| Region 3 | Asia (east of the Persian Gulf), Australia, New Zealand, and the Pacific west of 160° E |

Each region's amateur allocation is similar but not identical. The ITU "World Radiocommunication Conference" (WRC) sets the allocations through international treaty.

## Key amateur-band differences by region

### 160 m

- **Region 1**: 1.810-2.000 MHz amateur exclusive in some countries (e.g., UK only 1.810-2.000); often shared with shortwave broadcasting in Eastern Europe.
- **Region 2**: 1.800-2.000 MHz (US / Canada). Full 200 kHz amateur.
- **Region 3**: 1.800-2.000 MHz typical, but country-specific power and band-edge restrictions vary widely.

DX implication: a US 160 m station may be calling at 1.825 MHz, but a European DX-pedition may be limited to listening in 1.840-1.850. Hence split operation is common.

### 80 m

- **Region 1**: 3.500-3.800 MHz (no 3.800-4.000 in most of Region 1; some countries have segments).
- **Region 2**: 3.500-4.000 MHz (US gets the full 500 kHz).
- **Region 3**: 3.500-3.900 MHz typical (varies by country).

This is why 80 m DX often happens in the lower portion (3.500-3.800) — that's where Europeans can transmit. US operators looking for European DX usually call there.

### 60 m

- **Region 1**: 5.351.5-5.366.5 MHz amateur secondary (15 kHz wide, since WRC-2015).
- **Region 2**: US has 5 specific channels (covered in §18-01); Canada has the 5.3-5.4 MHz amateur secondary.
- **Region 3**: similar to Region 1 in some countries; varies widely.

The 60 m amateur band is **incomplete** — different countries have different access. Cross-region 60 m DX is rare.

### 40 m

- **Region 1**: 7.000-7.200 MHz (was historically only 7.000-7.100 in many countries; expanded at WRC-2003).
- **Region 2**: 7.000-7.300 MHz.
- **Region 3**: 7.000-7.300 MHz.

In Region 1, the upper portion (7.200-7.300) is shared with shortwave broadcasting; some European countries restrict amateur use there.

### 30 m, 20 m, 17 m, 15 m, 12 m, 10 m

These are mostly the **same allocations worldwide**:

- 30 m: 10.100-10.150 MHz (CW/digital only worldwide).
- 20 m: 14.000-14.350 MHz.
- 17 m: 18.068-18.168 MHz.
- 15 m: 21.000-21.450 MHz.
- 12 m: 24.890-24.990 MHz.
- 10 m: 28.000-29.700 MHz.

License-class restrictions vary; voluntary mode plans are coordinated by IARU regional working groups.

### 6 m and VHF

- **Region 1**: 50.000-52.000 MHz; some countries have 50.000-54.000 like US/Region 2.
- **Region 2**: 50.000-54.000 MHz.
- **Region 3**: 50.000-54.000 MHz typical (varies).

Region 1 amateurs typically have less 6 m allocation. This affects DX operating: a European 6 m operator may be limited to 50.000-52.000, so working European DX on 6 m happens in that segment.

### 2 m

- **Region 1**: 144.000-146.000 MHz (note: 2 MHz, not 4 MHz like US).
- **Region 2**: 144.000-148.000 MHz.
- **Region 3**: 144.000-148.000 MHz typical.

European 2 m is half as wide as US 2 m. This is why European 2 m repeater pairs use 600 kHz offsets in a tighter band.

### 70 cm

- **Region 1**: 430.000-440.000 MHz (10 MHz, vs. 30 MHz in Region 2).
- **Region 2**: 420.000-450.000 MHz.
- **Region 3**: 430.000-440.000 MHz typical.

Significantly narrower in Region 1. Repeater pairs use 1.6 MHz offsets in much of Europe (vs. 5 MHz in US Region 2).

## Voluntary band plan differences

Beyond the allocation differences, voluntary mode plans differ between regions:

### IARU Region 1 vs Region 2 conventions

- **Region 1**: heavier use of CW segments at the bottom of bands; heavier use of digital modes (especially in Eastern Europe where CW remains popular).
- **Region 2**: more use of SSB voice in the mid-band; FT8 has become dominant in the digital sub-bands.
- **Region 3**: similar to Region 2 but with country-specific quirks.

The differences are small but notable. A US operator listening for European stations on 20 m may find them in slightly different conventional segments than American stations.

## Country-specific quirks

### United Kingdom

- Some restricted bands (e.g., 4 m at 70.000-70.500 MHz) — a band that doesn't exist in the US.
- 5 m (60 MHz region) — certain countries.
- Different power limits per band per license class.

### Japan

- License classes are by output power tiers (1st Class through 4th Class).
- 1.9 MHz amateur secondary band (much narrower than US 160 m).
- Active 3 cm and higher microwave amateur community.

### Australia

- Full Amateur (A class) has very similar privileges to US Extra.
- 60 m secondary access expanded recently.
- Distinct 4 m band (70 MHz region) like UK.

### Russia / Former Soviet Union

- Active CW culture; many strong CW operators.
- Equivalent to Region 1 plus some country-specific extensions.

### China

- Limited HF amateur access for new operators.
- Strict QSL/log requirements for some bands.

## Why DX-peditions operate split

DX-peditions (amateur expeditions to rare countries — Bouvet Island, Heard Island, Crozet Islands) often operate **split** — transmitting on one frequency, listening on a different (higher or lower) frequency. There are several reasons:

1. **Their local rules differ from yours.** A DX-pedition operator from Region 1 may be limited to 14.000-14.150 SSB but able to listen elsewhere; you're transmitting in the General phone segment.
2. **The pile-up** — hundreds of stations calling at once. Splitting separates the calling stations from the DX-pedition's transmission so the DX op can hear individual callers.
3. **Pile-up rate-limiting** — a split operation lets the DX op announce "listening 200-220" and have the operators tune through that range; helps spread out the calls.

Standard procedure when working a split DX-pedition:

1. **Hear the DX** transmitting on a single frequency.
2. **Note the listening segment** — typically announced ("Up 5 to up 15") or implied by where stations are calling.
3. **Tune your transmitter to a frequency in the listening segment**, separate from your receive frequency.
4. **Call CQ on your TX frequency** while listening to the DX on RX.
5. When the DX answers your callsign, exchange RST and confirmation.

This is why "Listening 14.020" appears in DX-pedition info pages — it means the DX is on 14.020 RX, and you should TX in the segment they listen on (often 5+ kHz up).

## Power limits by region

US: 1500 W PEP. Most other regions: lower.

| Region/Country | Typical max amateur power |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| US | 1500 W PEP |
| Canada | 1500 W PEP |
| UK | 400 W ERP (Full); 50 W (Intermediate); 10 W (Foundation) |
| Germany | 750 W (DM and DJ classes); lower for Class E |
| Japan | 200 W (1 kW for some special licenses) |
| Australia | 400 W (Advanced); 100 W (Standard); 10 W (Foundation) |
| China | 1000 W typical Class A |

This affects DX exchanges: a US station running 1500 W into a beam may overwhelm a low-power station overseas. Operating habits should consider the other side's likely capabilities.

## Cross-band considerations for DX

In addition to allocation differences, **propagation favors specific paths and times**:

- **Trans-Atlantic DX (US ↔ Europe)**: typically late afternoon to early morning local time on 20 m, 17 m, 15 m, 10 m.
- **Trans-Pacific DX (US ↔ Asia)**: often best on greyline times; 20 m and 15 m most active.
- **South American DX**: easier from Florida than from California; reverse is also true (north-south paths).
- **Polar paths (US to Russia, Asia)**: heavily affected by geomagnetic storms.

Knowing the geography and the best propagation windows is part of working DX successfully.
