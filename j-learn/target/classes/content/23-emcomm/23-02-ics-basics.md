# ICS Basics for Amateur Operators

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

The **Incident Command System (ICS)** is the standardized framework used by U.S. emergency response organizations — federal, state, local, tribal — for managing any incident, from a single-vehicle accident up through nationwide disasters. When amateur radio supports a real emergency response, you are operating *within* an ICS structure. Understanding how ICS works, where you fit in, and what the served agency expects from you is more important than your technical radio skills.

This section is the orientation for amateur operators new to ICS. Full ICS training is available free from FEMA (IS-100, IS-200, IS-700, IS-800 — recommended baseline for any ARES/RACES volunteer). This section condenses what you need to know to be useful in an active deployment.

## What ICS is for

ICS exists to solve a recurring problem: when an emergency happens, multiple agencies show up — police, fire, EMS, public health, utilities, Red Cross, FEMA. Each has its own command structure, terminology, and operating procedures. Without a unifying framework, they trip over each other, lose situational awareness, and respond inefficiently.

ICS provides:

- **A common organizational structure** that scales from a one-person event to a thousands-of-personnel operation.
- **Common terminology** that means the same thing across agencies.
- **Defined roles** with specific responsibilities and authorities.
- **A documented planning cycle** (the "operational period").
- **A formal way to integrate auxiliary resources** — including amateur radio.

Every U.S. federal grant after the 2003 NIMS (National Incident Management System) reform has required ICS adoption. Every emergency-response agency in the country runs ICS. If you're activated for a real event, you're operating in ICS whether you know it or not.

## The ICS organizational chart

ICS has five major functional areas, each headed by a Section Chief reporting to the Incident Commander:

```
                Incident Commander (IC)
                        |
        ┌───────┬───────┼───────┬───────────┐
    Command   Liaison  PIO   Safety        |
    Staff     Officer  Officer Officer    |
                                          |
        ┌──────────┬─────────┬───────────┴─────┐
    Operations  Planning  Logistics    Finance/Admin
        |
   ┌────┴────┐
  Branches  Single Resources
  Divisions
  Groups
```

The five sections:

| Section | Role |
|---------|------|
| **Operations** | The people doing the actual work — fire crews, search teams, medical, security |
| **Planning** | Tracks resources, gathers and distributes information, develops the Incident Action Plan |
| **Logistics** | Provides everything Operations needs — shelter, food, equipment, medical for responders, **communications** |
| **Finance/Administration** | Tracks costs, processes claims, manages contracts |
| **Command Staff** | Directly supports the IC: Public Information Officer (media), Liaison Officer (other agencies), Safety Officer |

Amateur radio communications support is **typically a Logistics function** — specifically, part of the Communications Unit within the Logistics Section. The unit's leader is the **Communications Unit Leader (COML)**, sometimes a paid emergency manager, sometimes a volunteer with extensive training.

For small events, the structure compresses (one person handles multiple roles); for large events, it expands (Operations grows branches, divisions, and strike teams). The framework scales.

## Where amateur operators fit in

In a typical disaster response with amateur radio support, an amateur volunteer's ICS placement might be:

- **Communications Unit (Logistics)**: an amateur supporting overall communications planning and resource tracking.
- **Operational support**: an amateur deployed to a shelter, hospital, or field unit to provide that location's external communications.
- **Net control**: an amateur running the regional radio net that coordinates volunteers.
- **Liaison** (rare): an amateur connecting served agency to other amateur networks.

The chain of command:

You report to → **Net Control / Communications Unit Leader** → **Logistics Section Chief** → **Incident Commander**.

Practical implication: **you do what the served agency's incident commander needs** through this chain. You don't decide what the priority is; the IC does. Your job is to provide reliable communications support so the IC's orders can be carried out.

## ICS principles you'll encounter

### Unity of command

You report to **one** supervisor at a time. If the served agency tells you to do something but your ARES Emergency Coordinator tells you something different, your active assignment determines who you listen to. Confusion here is a common failure mode in early-deployment scenarios.

### Span of control

Each supervisor has 3-7 direct reports. If you're supervising more than 7 amateur operators, ICS expects you to subdivide.

### Common terminology

ICS avoids agency-specific jargon. Don't say "we have a 10-31 in progress"; say "burglary in progress." Amateurs adopt this convention when integrating with other ICS-trained personnel.

### Integrated communications

Communications are managed centrally within the Logistics section. The "Communications Plan" (ICS Form 205) lists every frequency, every callsign, every channel for the operation. Amateur radio is part of this plan when active.

### Operational period

ICS works in defined operational periods (typically 12 hours). At the start of each period, an **Incident Action Plan (IAP)** is briefed; everyone knows the goals for that period. Amateurs deploy to specific roles for the operational period.

### Modular organization

The structure expands and contracts as the incident requires. A small fire might have an IC who is also handling Operations and Planning personally; a large event has dozens of personnel in each section.

## Common ICS forms you might encounter

| Form | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| ICS-201 | Incident Briefing — initial summary at the start |
| ICS-202 | Incident Objectives — goals for this operational period |
| ICS-203 | Organization Assignment List — who's in what role |
| ICS-204 | Assignment List — specific tasks for a Group/Division |
| ICS-205 | Communications Plan — frequencies, channels, callsigns |
| ICS-206 | Medical Plan — medical for responders |
| ICS-209 | Incident Status Summary — situation update |
| ICS-211 | Check-In/Check-Out Log — track personnel |
| ICS-213 | General Message — formal traffic; widely used by amateur operators |
| ICS-214 | Activity Log — your time on duty, what you did |
| ICS-215 | Operational Planning Worksheet — for Planning Section |

For amateur operators, **ICS-205 (Communications Plan)** and **ICS-213 (General Message)** are the most-used. The 205 tells you what frequencies to operate on; the 213 is the standard "formal message" form for radio traffic.

## Training: the FEMA IS courses

The FEMA Emergency Management Institute (training.fema.gov) offers free online ICS training:

| Course | Topic | Time |
|--------|-------|------|
| IS-100 | Introduction to ICS | ~3 hours |
| IS-200 | ICS for Single Resources and Initial Action Incidents | ~5 hours |
| IS-700 | National Incident Management System (NIMS) Introduction | ~3 hours |
| IS-800 | National Response Framework | ~3 hours |

Most ARES groups expect IS-100 minimum; many require IS-100 + IS-200 + IS-700. Some require all four. Each is online, self-paced, free, and ends with a certificate that's tracked by FEMA — your local group can verify completion.

For more advanced training (Type 2 IC, COML, etc.), additional courses exist. Most amateurs don't need them; some volunteer Communications Unit Leaders do.

## What ICS doesn't tell you (but you need to know)

- **You're a guest.** The served agency is in charge. You provide a service; you don't direct the response.
- **Your role is communications.** Don't pick up search-and-rescue or medical tasks unless explicitly asked. Stay in your lane.
- **Integration takes practice.** First-time deployers often try to operate "as an amateur" and don't fit cleanly into ICS. This causes friction. Drill regularly with your local group to learn the integration patterns.
- **Documentation matters.** Every assignment, every message, every shift change needs an entry in the ICS-214 log. The records are part of the official incident record.

## ICS-213 and amateur traffic

The ICS-213 General Message form is the standard "formal traffic" within ICS. It has a defined structure: from, to, subject, message, response, signature. When amateur operators relay messages between agency personnel, the message typically uses an ICS-213.

Format and content are different from NTS radiograms (§23-01). Both can be transported via amateur radio, but the format and procedure differ.

NBEMS (Fldigi-based digital messaging) supports ICS-213 transmission directly: the form is filled out on screen, transmitted via Fldigi over the radio, and reconstructed at the receiving end.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** ICS terminology has specific definitions that diverge from common English. "Resource" = any person, equipment, or supply. "Type" = capability rating (Type 1 is most-capable; Type 5 is least). "Strike Team" = same-type resources working together. "Task Force" = mixed-type resources working together. The full glossary runs hundreds of terms; FEMA's ICS Resource Center has a complete reference. For amateur operators, IS-100 covers the essential vocabulary.

## Common ICS mistakes for new amateur operators

- **Trying to "command" the situation.** You're support, not command. Wait for instructions.
- **Skipping check-in.** Always check in formally with the Incident Command Post or your assigned supervisor before starting operations. Always check out at the end of your shift.
- **Bypassing the chain of command.** Going directly to the IC because you have a great idea = bad form. Tell your supervisor; if escalation is needed, they handle it.
- **Wrong terminology.** Calling a fire department's "engine" a "truck" can cause confusion (in ICS, those are different things). Use ICS terminology when speaking with non-amateur personnel.
- **No ICS-214 log.** Document. Sign in, sign out, log every action. The log may be reviewed days/weeks later for after-action review.
