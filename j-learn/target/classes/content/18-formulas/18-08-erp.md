# ERP / EIRP — Effective Radiated Power

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

ERP is the effective radiated power "felt" in the direction of an antenna's main lobe. It accounts for transmitter power, feedline loss, and antenna gain. ERP is what regulators care about for RF safety (§06) and what other operators care about for signal strength comparisons.

Two flavors:
- **ERP** is referenced to a half-wave dipole (gain in dBd).
- **EIRP** is referenced to an isotropic radiator (gain in dBi).

The two differ by a constant: 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi, so EIRP is always ~2.15 dB higher than the corresponding ERP.

## Equation

```
ERP(W)  = P_TX(W) × G_dipole × L_feedline
EIRP(W) = P_TX(W) × G_isotropic × L_feedline
```

In dB:

```
ERP(dBW)  = P_TX(dBW) + G_ant(dBd) − loss(dB)
EIRP(dBW) = P_TX(dBW) + G_ant(dBi) − loss(dB)
```

## Variables

| Symbol | Quantity | Units |
|--------|----------|-------|
| P_TX | Transmitter output power | W or dBW |
| G_ant | Antenna gain | linear or dB (dBd or dBi) |
| L_feedline | Feedline loss factor | linear (0..1) or dB |
| ERP | Effective radiated power vs. dipole | W or dBW |
| EIRP | Effective radiated power vs. isotropic | W or dBW |

## Conversion: linear ↔ dB

```
G(linear) = 10^(G(dB) / 10)
G(dB)     = 10 × log₁₀(G(linear))
```

## Dipole and isotropic relationship

```
G(dBi) = G(dBd) + 2.15
G(dBd) = G(dBi) − 2.15
```

In other words, a 0 dBd dipole has 2.15 dBi gain over an isotropic radiator (the dipole has gain because it doesn't radiate perfectly in all directions).

## Worked example — 100 W into a 6 dBd Yagi with 1 dB feedline loss

Linear:

```
P_TX = 100 W
G = 10^(6/10) = 3.98×       (gain factor)
L = 10^(−1/10) = 0.794×      (loss factor; lose 21%)

ERP = 100 × 3.98 × 0.794 ≈ 316 W
EIRP = ERP × 10^(2.15/10) ≈ 519 W
```

dB form:

```
P_TX = 10 × log(100) = 20 dBW
G = 6 dBd
L = 1 dB
ERP = 20 + 6 − 1 = 25 dBW
   convert: 10^(25/10) = 316 W ✓
EIRP = 25 + 2.15 = 27.15 dBW ≈ 519 W ✓
```

So 100 W into a 6 dBd antenna with reasonable feedline gives **about 300 W ERP** in the main lobe — the directive gain triples your effective signal in that direction.

## Worked example — 5 W HT with 3 dBi gain antenna

```
P_TX = 5 W
G = 3 dBi → 3 − 2.15 = 0.85 dBd
L = ~0 (HT, no feedline)

EIRP = 5 W × 10^(3/10) ≈ 5 × 2.0 ≈ 10 W
ERP  = 10 W − 2.15 dB ≈ 6.1 W
```

A 5 W HT with a gain antenna effectively puts out about 10 W EIRP — not 5, not 50.

## When does ERP / EIRP matter for RF safety?

For §06 RF-safety calculations, the **EIRP** is what's plugged into the Friis equation to compute power density at distance:

```
S = EIRP / (4π · d²)
```

(Power density in watts per square meter, given EIRP in watts and distance in meters.)

This is the formula that drives §18-14's RF-exposure worked example.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing dBi and dBd.** The 2.15 dB difference is constant. Always note which convention an antenna's gain spec uses.
- **Treating feedline loss as antenna gain.** Feedline losses subtract from output before the antenna; antenna gain is on top of *what reaches the antenna*. They're separate factors.
- **Forgetting that ERP is *directional*.** ERP is the power in the main lobe of the antenna pattern. Power off the back of a Yagi is much lower than ERP would suggest. RF-safety calcs use the worst-case (main-lobe) direction for distance to the user's body.
- **Using PEP for steady-state ERP.** SSB PEP is the peak; ERP for RF-safety averaging uses average power times duty cycle, not PEP. See §06-04 for duty-cycle correction.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** "Effective radiated power" is sometimes defined relative to dBi (especially in commercial-broadcast standards) and sometimes relative to dBd (FCC §97 ham-radio context). Always check the definition in the regulation you're working with. The FCC's RF-exposure rules use both ERP and EIRP, but the threshold tables (§06-02) explicitly state which one applies.
