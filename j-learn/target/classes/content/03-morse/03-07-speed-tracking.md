# Speed Tracking

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

CW progress is hard to feel day-to-day. Some sessions go great; some are terrible. Without measurement you'll either think you're stuck (when you're not) or push past your limits (when you shouldn't). A simple weekly speed-tracking habit fixes both problems.

## What to measure

Two numbers matter:

1. **Receive speed (effective WPM)** — the highest effective speed at which you can copy 90%+ accuracy on a 5-minute random-character test.
2. **Send speed (target WPM)** — the speed at which you can send a paragraph cleanly with no errors and no stuttering.

Receive speed is what people usually mean by "I'm at 18 WPM." Send speed lags receive speed by 5–10 WPM for most operators (you can copy faster than you can cleanly produce).

## How to test receive speed

Once a week, run a single test:

1. **Pick a trainer** — LCWO works well; G4FON also fine. Use the **same trainer** every week so the test conditions are consistent.
2. **Set character speed to 20 WPM** (don't change this; only change effective speed).
3. **Set effective speed to your current target** — start at 5 WPM, bump up over time.
4. **Run a 5-minute test of random text** with all characters you've learned.
5. **Count your accuracy.** Above 90%? Bump effective speed by 1 WPM next week. Below 90%? Hold the current speed for another week.
6. **Record the result** — date, character speed, effective speed, accuracy %.

Five minutes once a week. Skip it if you must, but don't fudge it — the value is in the trend.

## How to test send speed

Once a week, send a known paragraph at increasing speeds:

```
the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog
```

Or for amateur radio relevance:

```
cq de wm3j tnx fer call ur rst 599 name mike qth md
```

Send it five times at your target speed. Listen back to your sidetone. If all five are clean (no errors, no stutters, even spacing), you've earned that speed. If not, drop 2 WPM and re-test next week.

## Recording your progress

A simple text file is plenty. Or just a notebook page. The format doesn't matter; the consistency does.

```
Date         Receive (WPM)   Send (WPM)   Notes
2026-01-04   8 effective     5            Bumped from 7 last week, holding 92%
2026-01-11   8               6            Send getting smoother
2026-01-18   9               6            Receive bumped, send held
2026-01-25   9               7            
2026-02-01   10              7            
2026-02-08   10              7            Plateau on receive — sticky character: G
2026-02-15   10              8            Drilled G all week, broke through
...
```

Once you can graph this and see the line tilting up, the daily ups and downs stop bothering you. Even a flat week is fine — sometimes the brain consolidates without showing visible progress.

## What "bump the speed" means in practice

Each WPM increase is a real cognitive jump. Don't bump speed on the *one* good session you have — bump it after a *week* of solid 90%+ at the current speed.

When you do bump:

1. Go up 1 WPM, not 2.
2. Expect accuracy to drop to 70–80% for a few days.
3. Keep at it; accuracy returns to 90% in 1–2 weeks.
4. Then bump again.

If you tried to bump and accuracy stays below 80% for 3+ weeks, drop back 1 WPM. That's normal — sometimes the brain isn't ready and pushing it hurts.

## Plateaus and what to do about them

Every CW student hits plateaus. The most common ones:

| Plateau | When it happens | Why | What helps |
|---------|-----------------|-----|------------|
| 12 WPM | Around month 2 | Mental translation step ("translate dits and dahs to a letter") becomes the bottleneck | Keep character speed at 20+ WPM via Farnsworth; trust the Koch method |
| 18 WPM | Around month 4 | Switching from "letter copy" to "word copy" mode | Drill common short words (THE, AND, FOR, ARE) in dedicated sessions |
| 22 WPM | Several months in | Same skill jump, harder this time | Listen to fast on-air QSOs; try copy-by-ear (no writing) |
| 28 WPM | Years for most | Approaching real-time English speech rate | Long, dedicated effort; CWops Advanced classes; daily on-air contacts |

Most amateur CW operators settle at 20–25 WPM and operate happily there forever. There's nothing magical about speeds above 25 — many DX-peditions and contests run at 25 because that's where most of the population is.

## Track your weak characters

Beyond raw speed, track which characters give you trouble. A simple notebook entry:

```
Week of Feb 1:  G is sticky - confusing with Q
Week of Feb 8:  G is fine; now 5 vs H is a problem
Week of Feb 15: 5/H drilled, now solid
```

Most trainers have an "error analysis" mode that shows you which characters you got wrong. LCWO produces a per-character accuracy chart — extremely useful for targeted drilling.

## Stretch goals beyond raw speed

Once you're comfortable at 18–20 WPM, the next skills aren't about raw speed:

- **Copy-in-head (CIH)** — copying without writing anything down. The brain holds 5–10 characters in short-term memory. Forces you to *understand* what's being sent rather than transcribe.
- **Two-way QSOs at speed** — answering questions, asking questions, ad-libbing. Different from passive copy.
- **Pile-up calling** — picking out individual callsigns when many stations are calling at once.
- **Contesting** — high-rate exchange with short, predictable formats. Different mental skill from rag-chewing.
- **Headcopy of running text** — copying a paragraph of plain English at 25+ WPM in your head, with no notes.

Track these as separate skills. Some operators focus on CIH and never push for raw speed; others go the contest route and hit 35+ WPM via short-format-only practice.

## Time on the air vs time in the trainer

Once you're at 15 WPM you can have real on-air QSOs. Time in actual contacts is more valuable than equivalent time in a trainer for two reasons:

1. **Real callsigns and formats** are messier than trainer text.
2. **Real QSB and noise** force your brain to fill in missing characters from context — the most important real-world skill.

A typical balance once you're operational:

- 15–20 minutes/day of trainer practice (still building speed and clearing weak characters)
- 30+ minutes/day of on-air listening or QSOs (pattern recognition in real conditions)

Without on-air time, your trainer scores keep climbing but your real-world QSO ability stalls. With it, you grow as an *operator*, not just a copyist.

## When to stop tracking

After about a year of regular operation, formal speed tracking becomes less useful. By then you've found your comfortable speed (probably 18–25 WPM), you have stable habits, and progress comes from *operating* rather than from *training*. Stop the weekly tests if they feel like a chore. Pick them back up if you want to push past a new plateau.
