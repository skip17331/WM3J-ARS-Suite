# NVIS — Near-Vertical-Incidence Skywave

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

## The problem NVIS solves

Picture this. You're running an 80 m statewide emergency net in Pennsylvania. Your dipole at 50 ft does a beautiful job working DX in Europe at night — but right now, at noon, you can't hear the station 80 miles away in Pittsburgh. Ground wave on 80 m runs out around 30 miles. The F-layer skip from a low-angle antenna lands hundreds of miles away. There's a **skip zone** in between — the band-of-silence around your station where signals are too far for ground wave and too close for normal skywave. Your net is fractured because half the stations are in each other's skip zone.

**NVIS** — Near-Vertical-Incidence Skywave — fixes this. You shoot signals nearly *straight up*. The ionosphere bends them back down within a 0–300 mile radius around the transmitter. There is no skip zone: the signal lands everywhere under the umbrella, including right at your antenna's feet.

## What NVIS is, physically

The F-layer (and during daytime, the F1 + E layers below it) reflects HF signals only up to a certain critical frequency, **foF2**, which varies by location, time of day, and solar conditions. Below foF2, signals striking the ionosphere at high incidence angles — including 90° (straight up) — reflect back down. The reflection geometry means the signal lands roughly at the transmitter's location, with the energy spread out in a circle whose radius depends on the angle at which the signal left your antenna.

| Takeoff angle | Approximate footprint radius |
|----------------|------------------------------|
| 90° (straight up) | Footprint directly under your antenna; nearly zero hop distance |
| 75° | ~50 miles |
| 60° | ~150 miles |
| 45° | ~300 miles |
| 30° | ~500–700 miles (regular skywave begins here) |

The NVIS sweet spot is **roughly 60°–90° takeoff** — what you get from a horizontal dipole at less than 1/4 wavelength above ground.

## Frequency matters — pick below foF2

NVIS only works if your operating frequency is below foF2. On a typical mid-latitude midday, foF2 hangs around 5–8 MHz; at night it drops to 3–4 MHz. So:

| Band | Daytime NVIS? | Nighttime NVIS? |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| **80 m (3.5–4.0 MHz)** | Often marginal at midday | Good |
| **60 m (5.3–5.4 MHz)** | Good | Sometimes; depends on foF2 |
| **40 m (7.0–7.3 MHz)** | Good when foF2 > 7 MHz | Usually too high — signal escapes |

Practical rule: **80 m at night, 40 m during the day**. 60 m, where authorized, is the all-day NVIS channel and is why it was originally allocated to amateurs (emcomm work).

## The antenna — low and horizontal

The conventional advice — "get your antenna up high" — is wrong for NVIS. You want the antenna *low*:

- **Height:** λ/8 to λ/4 above ground. For 80 m, that's 30–60 ft. For 40 m, 15–30 ft.
- **Type:** A simple horizontal dipole oriented broadside to nothing in particular (footprint is roughly circular at very low heights).
- **Optional reflector wire:** A second wire laid on the ground under the dipole, slightly longer (about 5%) than the dipole, acts as a ground reflector and adds a few dB to the upward-pointing pattern. This is the classic NVIS dipole-over-counterpoise configuration used by Army MARS.

A higher dipole *loses* NVIS performance because the pattern develops a vertical null straight up and lobes at lower angles. A 40-ft 80 m dipole is a better NVIS antenna than the same wire at 80 ft. This is one of the few cases in amateur radio where "lower is better."

> **Advanced —** The takeoff-angle pattern of a horizontal dipole is a function of dipole height in wavelengths. At h = λ/4 the pattern peaks at zenith (90°). At h = λ/2 the pattern has a null at zenith and lobes at ~30°. At h = λ the pattern has multiple lobes with a strong null at 90°. For NVIS, you want h ≤ λ/4 — which on 80 m is 60 ft or less. The reflector wire on the ground roughly doubles the upward radiated power vs. dipole alone, but a real-ground modeled gain is more like +2 to +3 dB rather than the +6 dB you'd get over a perfect ground plane.

## Practical operating — the East-Coast 80 m daytime nets

The classic NVIS scenario in the US is a statewide net on 80 m mid-day. Example: 3.873 MHz on the East Coast, mid-morning to early afternoon. Stations from Maine to Virginia check in; signals are S5–S9 across the entire net coverage, no skip zone, no dead spots.

A typical net schedule:

```
0900 EST — Net Control calls "WM3J Pennsylvania ARES net, NCS K3ABC, please check in"
0901      — Check-ins from PA, NJ, MD, DE, VA, NY, CT, MA — all S5+ on NVIS
0930      — Tactical traffic passed: shelter status, weather, ARES assignments
0950      — Net secures
```

The same net on a high low-takeoff-angle dipole would lose half the stations to the skip zone. With NVIS-optimized antennas at all ends, every check-in is solid copy.

## When NVIS shines

- **Statewide emcomm.** ARES/RACES nets after a regional disaster (hurricane, ice storm) where commercial infrastructure is down and FM repeaters are off the air.
- **Tactical military / disaster comms.** US Army manpack HF radios use NVIS by default — the antenna deploys at 6–8 ft over a counterpoise.
- **Inter-county Field Day coordination.** Multi-site Field Day groups talking to each other 50–200 miles apart.
- **Regional traffic nets** in MARS or NTS where the goal is to relay traffic across one state, not span the continent.
- **Quiet rural QSO target.** If you're trying to reach a buddy 100 miles away and 2 m simplex won't make it, NVIS on 80 m or 40 m will.

## When NVIS doesn't help

- **You want DX.** NVIS is the opposite of DX. Raise the antenna for long-haul work.
- **The band is above foF2.** 40 m at night, 20 m anytime — your signal goes through the ionosphere and never comes back at NVIS angles. Use the right band for the time of day.
- **The band is closed.** During severe geomagnetic storms (Kp ≥ 7), even NVIS frequencies can fail. Have a VHF/UHF fallback.

## Tactical NVIS gear

The "deployable" NVIS station is a small subculture of amateur radio with overlap into emcomm:

- **Manpack radio:** Yaesu FT-857, Icom IC-705, Elecraft KX2/KX3, or military surplus PRC-104/150 series.
- **Wire dipole on glass-fiber masts** at 12–15 ft. Two telescoping fishing-pole masts and 130 ft of wire is enough.
- **Tuner** if running a non-resonant wire — most field NVIS uses cut-to-band dipoles to avoid tuner losses.
- **Battery** — LiFePO4 12 V, 15–30 Ah, runs a 100 W rig for hours of net work.

The whole station fits in a backpack and deploys in 15 minutes — which is exactly the point.

> **Advanced —** The Army's AN/PRC-150 and successor radios include an Automatic Link Establishment (ALE) feature that scans NVIS frequencies and probes the ionosphere with brief tones to pick the best one in real time. Amateur ALE on 60 m and 80 m exists (PCALE software) but is rare. ALE is the operational answer to "foF2 is changing all day" — instead of guessing, the radio measures.
