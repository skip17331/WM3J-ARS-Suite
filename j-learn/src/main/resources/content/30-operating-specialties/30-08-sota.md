# SOTA — Summits On The Air

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

## What it is

**Summits On The Air (SOTA)** is a worldwide amateur-radio award program where operators hike to mountain summits, set up a portable station, and make four or more contacts from there. The summit gets credited as "activated." Other operators who make contact with the activator are "chasers." Both sides earn points based on the summit's elevation and bonus seasonal factors.

Started in 2002 in the UK (specifically by John GW4BVE), SOTA has grown into a global community with active associations in 100+ countries. The program database (`sotadata.org.uk`) tracks every activation, chase, and summit reference number.

## Why SOTA caught on

Three reasons SOTA is one of the most popular amateur portable activities today:

1. **It combines hiking and radio.** The outdoor crowd in amateur radio has somewhere structured to go.
2. **Low equipment threshold.** A 5 W QRP rig, an end-fed wire, and a small battery is enough. Total kit weight under 5 lbs.
3. **The point system makes it a game.** Activators earn points per summit (more for higher elevations); accumulating to "Mountain Goat" (1000 points) is a recognized milestone.

## Summit codes and references

Every SOTA-eligible summit has a unique reference number in the format `region/area-NNN`. Examples:

- `W2/GA-019` — New York, Greater Adirondacks area, summit 019 (Wright Peak)
- `G/LD-003` — Great Britain, Lake District, summit 003 (Scafell Pike)
- `EA1/NA-001` — Spain, Asturias, summit 001 (Torre Cerredo)

The first 1–2 letters are the **association** (W = USA, G = Great Britain, DL = Germany, etc.). The next letters are the **region** within the association. The number identifies the specific summit.

To qualify as a SOTA summit, the peak must:
- Be on a published SOTA list (associations curate these)
- Have a defined "activation zone" — usually within 25 m vertical of the true summit
- Be reached by physically moving (no driving to the top, with limited exceptions)

## Equipment for SOTA

A typical SOTA pack:

| Item | Typical product | Weight |
|------|----------------|--------|
| HF rig | Elecraft KX2, Yaesu FT-818, Xiegu G90 | 1–2 lb |
| Battery | LiFePO4 4-cell 12 V, 4–10 Ah | 1–2 lb |
| Antenna | End-fed half-wave (40m or 20m), or linked dipole | 0.5 lb |
| Mast | Carbon-fiber telescoping, 6–10 m | 1 lb |
| Logging | Paper logbook or phone app (HamLog, RuMlogNG) | 0 |
| Misc | Coax, paddles, headphones, key, stakes, water, snacks | 1 lb |

Total: 5–7 lb of radio kit. Adding hiking gear (water, clothes, food, navigation) gets you to a manageable 10–15 lb pack for a day hike.

## Operating practice

A SOTA activation typically goes:

1. **Hike to the summit** and confirm GPS coordinates against the published reference.
2. **Set up in the activation zone.** A mast supported by guy lines, the antenna deployed (often sloping wire), the rig set up on a sit pad.
3. **Spot yourself** on `sotawatch.sota.org.uk` via cellular if you have signal — chasers see the spot and tune to your frequency immediately.
4. **Call CQ:** `"CQ SOTA, CQ SOTA, this is Whiskey Mike Three Juliet portable, activating Wright Peak Whiskey-Two Golf-Alpha zero-one-nine, calling CQ SOTA."`
5. **Work chasers** — typically 10–30 contacts in 30–60 minutes if the band is good. Exchange the SOTA summit reference both ways.
6. **Log** each contact with time, callsign, band, mode, signal report, and the chaser's grid or location.
7. **Submit** the activation to the SOTA database via the web interface (usually within a few days). Points credit automatically.

## Common operating frequencies

SOTA activity is loosely distributed across CW and SSB on:

- **20 m** — 14.062 MHz CW, 14.342 MHz SSB (most common globally)
- **40 m** — 7.032 MHz CW, 7.185 MHz SSB
- **17 m** — 18.082 MHz CW (less crowded)
- **VHF** — 144.200 MHz USB (Europe), 146.520 MHz FM (US)

In the US, 14.342 and 14.062 are the watering holes.

## Scoring

Points per summit:

| Elevation (m) | Points |
|----------------|--------|
| 150–1000       | 1      |
| 1000–1500      | 2      |
| 1500–2500      | 4      |
| 2500–4000      | 6      |
| 4000+          | 10     |

Plus a "winter bonus" of +1 to +3 points if activated December–March in northern-hemisphere associations.

Chaser points are usually equal to the summit's value. So a 4-point activator and a 4-point chaser both score 4 for the QSO.

## Etiquette

- **Spot yourself.** Chasers can't find you otherwise. Use SOTAwatch or SOTAGoat (mobile app).
- **Call brief.** Each contact is 20–60 seconds. No ragchew during the activation.
- **Listen for chasers from across the world.** A 20 m signal from a summit can reach Europe from the US — don't dismiss European chasers because they sound far.
- **Acknowledge the summit reference** in every contact. Chasers need it for their log.
- **Don't activate dangerous summits in bad weather** just to earn points. Safety first.
