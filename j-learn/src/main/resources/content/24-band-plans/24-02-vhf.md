# VHF

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

VHF amateur bands cover three allocations: **6 m, 2 m, and 1.25 m**. All Technicians and higher have full privileges across these bands; there are no license-class sub-band restrictions in VHF amateur radio. The structure within each band is purely the **voluntary mode plan**, conventional repeater pairs, and segment conventions.

This is also the home of FM voice and most repeaters — the daily-driver bands for casual amateur communication.

## US VHF amateur allocations

| Band | Frequency range | Bandwidth |
|------|-----------------|-----------|
| 6 m | 50.0–54.0 MHz | 4 MHz |
| 2 m | 144.0–148.0 MHz | 4 MHz |
| 1.25 m | 222.0–225.0 MHz | 3 MHz |

All three bands are open to all license classes from Technician up.

## 6 m (50.0–54.0 MHz) — "The Magic Band"

The widest VHF band, 4 MHz wide. Behaves like HF in some openings (worldwide propagation during sporadic-E or solar flux peaks) and like VHF in others (line-of-sight only). Hence "magic" — surprises happen.

### Voluntary 6 m mode plan

| Frequency | Convention |
|-----------|-----------|
| 50.000-50.030 | CW (legal CW segment for low-power) |
| 50.060-50.080 | Beacons (NCDXF/IARU style; many regional beacons) |
| 50.080-50.100 | CW DX |
| 50.100-50.300 | SSB (USB) — DX window |
| 50.125 | SSB calling frequency (USB; international calling) |
| 50.260 | FT8 (de facto) — busy during 6 m openings |
| 50.276 | JS8Call (de facto) |
| 50.290-50.310 | Meteor scatter (FT8/MSK144 high-traffic) |
| 50.310-50.700 | Mixed; experimental, weak signal |
| 50.700-51.000 | Repeater inputs/outputs (regional varies) |
| 51.000-52.000 | FM simplex / repeaters |
| 52.000-53.000 | Repeater outputs (regional varies) |
| 53.000-54.000 | FM simplex; some repeater outputs |

### 6 m beacons

The IARU and individual amateurs operate beacons at fixed frequencies in the 50.060-50.080 range to indicate band openings. When the band is closed, beacons are silent (within line-of-sight); when sporadic-E or other propagation opens the band, multiple beacons can be heard at once.

### 6 m calling frequencies

- **50.125 USB** — international SSB calling (USB).
- **50.260** — FT8 calling.
- **52.525 FM** — national FM simplex calling (US).

When the band opens, hundreds of stations may converge on these frequencies.

## 2 m (144.0–148.0 MHz) — "The Workhorse Band"

The most-used amateur band overall — most amateurs spend more on-the-air time on 2 m than any other band. Repeaters are densely deployed; FM voice dominates; weak-signal modes occupy the lower edge.

### 2 m repeater pairs

Repeater offsets on 2 m in the US are conventionally:

- **Below 147 MHz** (146.0-146.99): negative offset (input is 600 kHz LOWER than output)
- **Above 147 MHz** (147.0-147.99): positive offset (input is 600 kHz HIGHER than output)

Example: A repeater output on 146.940 has its input on 146.340 (negative 600 kHz offset).

This is conventional only — some repeaters use non-standard splits (especially in mountainous regions with terrain-driven coordination needs).

### 2 m calling frequencies

- **146.520 FM** — national FM simplex calling. Where to call CQ when the repeaters are quiet.
- **144.200 SSB (USB)** — SSB weak-signal calling.
- **144.174 FT8** — digital weak-signal calling. Major activity during 2 m openings (sporadic-E, tropo).

## 1.25 m (222.0–225.0 MHz)

The narrow VHF band — 3 MHz wide. Less heavily used than 2 m or 70 cm. Some regions have active repeater communities; others have almost no activity.

### Voluntary 1.25 m mode plan

| Frequency | Convention |
|-----------|-----------|
| 222.000-222.150 | Weak signal (CW, SSB) |
| 222.000-222.025 | CW |
| 222.100 | SSB / CW calling |
| 222.150-222.250 | Beacons |
| 222.250-223.380 | Repeater inputs (regional) |
| 223.380-223.400 | Simplex calling |
| 223.400-223.520 | Simplex |
| 223.520-225.000 | Repeater outputs (regional) |

### 1.25 m calling frequencies

- **223.500 FM** — national FM simplex calling on 1.25 m.

## VHF mode behavior

A few notes on what each mode is good for at VHF:

### FM voice
The dominant mode. Repeaters everywhere. Squelch-based; either you're in or you're out. Range typically line-of-sight + slight tropospheric ducting.

### SSB (USB)
Used for weak-signal work. Range can extend hundreds of miles via tropospheric scatter, sporadic-E, or other propagation. The 144.200 calling frequency activates during band openings.

### CW
Lower edge of each band. Used for weak-signal work and beacons. Common in VHF DX-pedition operations.

### FT8
Widely used at VHF for weak-signal DX. 2 m FT8 (144.174) regularly logs cross-state contacts during openings; 6 m FT8 (50.260) catches transcontinental QSOs during sporadic-E.

### Meteor scatter
Specialized: pings off ionized meteor trails. Mostly 6 m and 2 m. Uses FT8/MSK144 with specific timing. The 50.290-50.310 segment is the meteor-scatter conventional area.

## Repeater linking

Many VHF repeaters link to other repeaters via:

- **AllStar Link** — VoIP-based linking.
- **EchoLink** — internet-linked simplex/repeater system.
- **DMR networks** — TG IDs that span continents.
- **D-STAR / YSF** — proprietary digital networks with reflectors.

A 2 m repeater in Texas can link to a 70 cm repeater in Tokyo via internet backbone. The voluntary mode plan still applies — operate in proper segments — but communication range becomes effectively global.

## Sporadic-E and tropo openings

VHF bands occasionally open beyond line-of-sight via:

- **Sporadic-E** (mostly 6 m, occasionally 2 m): patches of dense ionization in the E layer reflect VHF signals worldwide. Common summer afternoons.
- **Tropospheric ducting**: thermal inversions in the troposphere reflect VHF signals over hundreds of miles. Common spring/fall, especially over water.
- **Aurora**: scattered VHF signals during geomagnetic storms; characteristic "fluttery" sound.
- **Meteor scatter**: brief pings off meteor ionization.
- **EME (moonbounce)**: signals reflecting off the moon. Requires high power and high-gain antennas.

When these are active, calling frequencies (50.125, 144.200) are crowded.

## Common mistakes

- **Operating SSB in repeater segments.** SSB at 145.300 will not be received by FM repeater listeners at all (they're squelched on no carrier); but it interferes with linked digital systems and is generally the wrong segment.
- **Operating FM at 144.200.** Disruptive — that's the SSB calling frequency, where weak-signal stations are listening for marginal contacts.
- **Forgetting offset on repeater operation.** "I'm calling on 146.940" — yes, but if you're transmitting 146.940 you're transmitting the repeater output, not the input. Use proper repeater programming.
- **Calling CQ on 146.520 with PL tone.** The simplex calling frequency doesn't use a PL tone; using one means most listeners with default rigs (no PL set) won't hear you.
- **High power on 2 m FM.** 100 W FM mobile into a Yagi at 144 MHz is overkill for 99% of contacts; the energy goes into front-loading the repeater receiver desensitization circuits (and your rig's heatsink).
