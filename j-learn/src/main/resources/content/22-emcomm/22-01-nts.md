# NTS — National Traffic System

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

The **National Traffic System** is amateur radio's structured network for handling formal written messages — "radiograms" — over a hierarchy of regularly-scheduled nets. The system has operated continuously since 1949, daily, and serves as both the standing message-handling capability for amateur traffic and as a training platform for the operators who would handle traffic during an emergency.

NTS is what happens when "I want to send a message to grandma in Iowa" or "we need to relay 50 health-and-welfare messages out of a hurricane-stricken zone" needs to actually move through the radio network. The system is slow (minutes to hours per message under good conditions; days under disaster conditions) but reliable when nothing else is.

## What an NTS message is

A radiogram is a formal text message with a fixed structure:

- **Number**: a station-issued sequential number for tracking.
- **Precedence**: routine (R), welfare (W), priority (P), or emergency (EMERGENCY).
- **HX (handling instructions)**: optional codes for delivery requirements (HXA = collect calls, HXC = report delivery, etc.).
- **Station of origin**: the callsign of the operator who first put the message on the radio.
- **Check**: a count of the words in the text (the receiving station verifies they got all the words).
- **Place of origin**: where the message originated (city, state).
- **Time filed**: when the message entered the radio network (typically UTC).
- **Date**: month and day of origination.
- **Address**: the addressee — name, address, city, state, ZIP, phone (if known).
- **Text**: the actual message; up to 25 words is conventional.
- **Signature**: the originator's name (the person sending; not the radio operator).

A typical text might read: "ARRIVED SAFELY MOM AND DAD WANT YOU TO CALL X HOPE TO SEE YOU SOON LOVE STEVE"

(The "X" is a punctuation indicator; "X" in NTS text means a period or "stop.")

## The operating goal

NTS aims to move a message from any originator to any addressee in the U.S. (and points abroad through links) using only amateur radio. The system is **store-and-forward**: a message is handed off station-to-station, station-to-net, and net-to-net until it reaches a station near the addressee, who then delivers it (typically by phone, but historically by mail or in person).

For a routine traffic message: typical delivery time is 12-48 hours. For priority traffic: a few hours. For emergency: as fast as conditions allow.

## The hierarchy

NTS operates in a tiered structure, with each tier covering a larger geographic area:

| Level | Scope | Example |
|-------|-------|---------|
| Local nets | A city or county | Section-specific traffic nets, often on 2 m repeaters |
| Section nets | An ARRL section (typically a state) | "Maryland Slow Net," "Washington State Net" |
| Regional nets | A multi-state region | "Eastern Pennsylvania-Maryland-Delaware Region Net" |
| Area nets | One of three U.S. "areas" — Eastern, Central, Pacific | "Eastern Area Net," "Pacific Area Net" |
| Transcontinental Corps (TCC) | Inter-area liaison | TCC Eastern, TCC Central, TCC Pacific |

A message originating in Florida and addressed to a recipient in Oregon would typically:

1. Be relayed up from Florida local nets → Florida Section Net.
2. Section Net → Eastern Area Net.
3. Eastern Area Net → TCC Eastern.
4. TCC Eastern → TCC Pacific.
5. TCC Pacific → Pacific Area Net.
6. Pacific Area Net → Oregon Section Net.
7. Oregon Section Net → local Oregon delivery station.
8. Local station calls the addressee by phone.

Each step happens during a scheduled net. Typical net schedules are hourly or twice-daily; a message might pass through 3-4 nets to reach the destination, each net taking 10-30 minutes.

## Net structure

A typical NTS net runs as follows:

- **Net Control Station (NCS)** opens the net at the scheduled time on the published frequency.
- NCS calls for "check-ins" — operators report into the net, identifying their station and what traffic they have.
- NCS records who's checked in and what messages exist.
- Once check-ins are complete, NCS pairs stations: "WM3J, please listen up 5 for W1ABC who has a message for you."
- The two stations move to the side frequency, exchange the message, and return to the net.
- Process repeats until all traffic has been passed or the net's time has expired.
- NCS closes the net.

This is highly disciplined operating — much closer to a formal military or commercial network than a casual amateur QSO. Brevity and accuracy matter.

## Filling out the radiogram form

The ARRL's standard radiogram form has spaces for every preamble field. Originating operators fill it out before going on the air. A worked example:

```
┌─ Number ─┬─ Precedence ─┬─ HX ─┬─ Station of Origin ─┬─ Check ─┐
│   47     │      R       │      │       W1ABC          │   16    │
├──── Place of Origin ────┼─ Time Filed ─┼─ Date ──────┤
│      Newport RI         │    1430Z     │  NOV 15     │
└─────────────────────────┴──────────────┴─────────────┘

To:   John Smith
      123 Main Street
      Anytown VA 12345
      555-1234

ARRIVED SAFELY SUNDAY X WILL CALL YOU TUESDAY X
LOVE MARY

From: Mary Smith
      14 Bayview Road
      Newport RI 02840
```

Notes on the fields:

- **Number** is sequential and station-issued. Many operators reset to 1 each January; some never reset. The number lets you track this specific message in your log.
- **Precedence** is one letter (R/W/P) or the word EMERGENCY. Default is R.
- **HX (handling instructions)** is empty unless the originator specifies a code. Common ones:
  - HXA — collect-call delivery (if phone)
  - HXB — cancel if not delivered by [time]
  - HXC — report delivery to originator
  - HXD — report receipt and final delivery
  - HXE — delivering station: get reply from addressee, originate that as a return message
  - HXF — hold delivery until [date/time]
  - HXG — delivery by mail or phone toll call not required; cancel if not free
- **Station of origin** is the FCC callsign that first put the message on radio. Not the originator (the human who wrote it) — that goes in the signature.
- **Check** is the count of words in the text **only** (not the address or signature). Count "ARL" as one word; count "STOP" or "X" as one word; numbers spelled out count as one word ("ONE THOUSAND" = two words).
- **Place of origin** is the originator's location (where the message was written), not where it entered the network.
- **Time filed** is when the originator gave it to you, in UTC.
- **Date** is month + day in UTC.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** When the originator is the same person as the radio operator, "place of origin" is your QTH. When you're handling someone else's message (you wrote it down at a Red Cross shelter, for example), the place of origin is the shelter location and the signature is the shelter occupant's name. The check counts words including any "X" or "STOP" punctuation indicators, but excludes the BREAK markers and any optional segment separators. ARL numbered text is treated as a single token: "ARL FIFTY ONE" counts as 3 words.

## ARL numbered text

The **ARL numbered radiograms** are a set of pre-defined message templates, each tied to a number. They compress common message types into a few words and reduce both transmission time and copy errors.

```
ARL FIFTY  →  "Greetings by Amateur Radio. This message was sent free
              of charge by your local Amateur Radio operator..."

ARL FORTY SIX → "Greetings on your birthday and best wishes for many more
                 to come."

ARL SIXTY ONE → "Wish you were here."

ARL SIXTY SEVEN → "Greetings and best wishes for a Merry Christmas
                   and a Happy New Year."
```

Two groups exist:

- **Group One (1–45)**: emergency / disaster messages — "All safe here," "House damaged but family OK," "Need food and water," etc.
- **Group Two (46–69)**: routine / holiday messages — birthday, anniversary, holidays, congratulations, condolences.

Operating example:

```
Originator: "Send 'Happy birthday' to my mother."
Operator:   Writes the message text as "ARL FORTY SIX X JOHN"
            Check = 4 (ARL, FORTY, SIX, JOHN — X is punctuation in NTS,
                       counted as 1 word, so check = 5).
```

The receiving station looks up "ARL 46" in their reference card and delivers the full canonical text to the addressee.

ARL groups are defined in the ARRL's Numbered Radiograms list (publicly available). Most active traffic handlers carry a printed card; many radios display them. They speed up disaster traffic dramatically — a "we're safe; will call when phone restored" message becomes "ARL ONE" plus a signature.

## How a message is sent on the air

A typical exchange between two NTS operators (CW or voice; conventions are similar):

```
NCS: "WM3J this is W1ABC; I have one routine message"
WM3J: "Roger, ready to copy"
W1ABC: "Number 47 routine W1ABC check 16 Newport RI 1430Z November 15"
W1ABC: "John Smith"
W1ABC: "123 Main Street"
W1ABC: "Anytown VA 12345"
W1ABC: "Phone 555-1234"
W1ABC: "Break"
W1ABC: "Arrived safely Sunday X Will call you Tuesday X Mary"
W1ABC: "Break"
W1ABC: "End of message; over"
WM3J: "Roger; copied 47 from W1ABC; over"
W1ABC: "Roger; QRU; over"
WM3J: "Roger no more for you; clear"
```

The "BREAK" is a procedural marker between segments (preamble → address → text → signature). "X" within the text is a period. The check ("16") tells WM3J how many words to expect in the text, providing an integrity check.

If a word is missed, WM3J asks for "FILL" — typically the number of the missed word — and W1ABC repeats from that word forward.

## Traffic precedence

| Code | Meaning | Use |
|------|---------|-----|
| R (Routine) | Normal personal traffic | Birthday wishes, casual updates, tourist messages |
| W (Welfare) | Health-and-welfare during emergencies | "Family safe; no need to worry" |
| P (Priority) | Important business or urgent personal | Immediate but not life-threatening matters |
| EMERGENCY | Life-or-death | Severe injury, search and rescue, immediate medical |

In an emergency, lower-precedence traffic is held; higher-precedence is moved first. EMERGENCY traffic interrupts everything, including ongoing routine traffic.

## Practical NTS skills

The skills you develop in NTS:

- **Net discipline** — listening, waiting your turn, brief responses.
- **Message accuracy** — counting check, copying carefully, requesting fills when needed.
- **Phonetics and prosigns** — radiograms are filled with names, addresses, numbers; phonetic spelling is constant.
- **Speed and concision** — moving traffic efficiently without errors.
- **Familiarity with formal procedures** — useful in emergency settings where formal traffic handling is required.

These translate directly to disaster response work. Operators who practice in NTS daily are operators ready to handle emergency traffic when needed.

## Modern NTS adaptations

Traditional NTS uses voice (FM/SSB/CW). Modern adaptations:

- **NBEMS** (Narrow-Band Emergency Messaging System): Fldigi-based digital messaging, can transport ICS-213 forms and other structured traffic.
- **WinLink** (Radio Email): a separate but related radio email system, NOT the same as NTS but used for emergency message handling. WinLink uses email-format messages over RF.
- **ICS-213 over packet/Fldigi**: tactical messages in ICS format, transmitted via digital modes.

NTS hasn't gone away; it remains the formal-radiogram system. Digital modes have augmented it.

## NTS vs. tactical communications

NTS handles **formal traffic** — written messages with a specific structure, expected to reach a defined addressee. It's not the right tool for:

- **Tactical communications** during an active event ("Send the medical team to shelter B"). Tactical voice on a local repeater or simplex is faster and looser.
- **Health-and-welfare lists** generated en masse from a shelter. (Some events produce hundreds; NTS can handle them but the volume strains the system.)
- **Internal coordination** within an emergency operations center. EOC traffic is usually direct voice on local frequencies.

NTS specifically targets the "send a structured message that needs to be delivered" use case.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** The NTS routing structure is defined in the ARRL's Public Service Communications Manual and Operating Manual. Each NTS net has a specific call-up frequency, time, and net manager. Cross-net liaison is provided by appointed operators (Net Liaison stations) who check into multiple nets to relay traffic between them. The TCC (Transcontinental Corps) handles long-haul liaison via dedicated digital modes (PSK, Fldigi-MFSK) and CW; the modern TCC uses extensively-rehearsed nightly nets to move traffic between the three U.S. areas with minimal delay.

## Common NTS mistakes

- **Using NTS-formal procedures for casual messages.** Use 73 or a regular QSO for "happy birthday" type traffic; NTS is for messages that need to actually arrive somewhere.
- **Not counting the check.** The text word count is a critical integrity tool. Count carefully.
- **Inadequate phonetics.** "Smith" → "Sierra Mike India Tango Hotel"; do this for every name and address word.
- **Skipping the BREAK.** Breaks separate sections and give the receiver pacing markers.
- **Forgetting precedence.** A routine message during an emergency is OK; a routine message claiming priority is bad form.
- **Failing to deliver.** If you accept a message, you're responsible for it. If you can't reach the addressee, mark it "service" and return undelivered status.

## Getting started with NTS

1. **Find your local section's NTS schedule** — ARRL publishes section net frequencies and times.
2. **Check into a net** in listening mode for a session or two; observe procedures.
3. **Originate a simple message** to a friend in another state.
4. **Send it via the local net** with the help of an experienced traffic handler.
5. **Track delivery** — most receivers will confirm they delivered.

Local NTS net training is available almost everywhere; ask the section's Net Manager.
