# End-Fed Half-Wave (EFHW)

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

The end-fed half-wave wire is the modern wire-antenna favorite. **One support, one feedline run that doesn't have to come from the antenna's center, multi-band by design**, and a feed system simple enough to fit in a 4×4 box. Portable ops, attic installs, and crowded suburban yards all gravitate to the EFHW for the same reason: it solves layout problems no other antenna does.

It's not without compromises — every advertised feature comes with an asterisk — but the EFHW deserves its popularity.

## What it is

A half-wave-long wire, fed at one **end** rather than the center. The feedpoint impedance of a half-wave wire at its end is high — typically **2200–4500 Ω** depending on wire thickness, ground proximity, and exact length. To match this to 50 Ω coax, you use a transformer:

- **49:1 unun** (ratio 7:1 turns) — the most common choice. Steps 50 Ω up to 2450 Ω.
- **64:1 unun** (8:1 turns) — for installations where the actual feedpoint impedance trends higher (drier ground, smaller-diameter wire, very long horizontal runs).
- **56:1 unun** (between the two) — sometimes seen in commercial designs as a compromise.

The unun is wound on a high-permeability ferrite toroid (typically FT240-43 or FT140-43). Quality matters: a poorly-wound or undersized unun saturates at high power and adds significant loss.

## The classic EFHW: 80–10 m harmonic resonant

A wire **133 feet long** (a half-wavelength on 3.55 MHz) is also a multiple-of-half-wave on:

- 40 m (full wave)
- 20 m (2 waves)
- 15 m (3 waves)
- 10 m (4 waves)
- (17 m and 12 m are *not* harmonically related — those bands need an ATU.)

A single wire and a single 49:1 unun thus give you resonant operation on **5 bands**. With a tuner, all of 80–10 m. Add a small extra coil at the far end (a "loading inductor") and you can include 60 m as well.

This is the magic. **One wire, one feedline, five bands.** No center support needed. No traps. The closest equivalent in any other family is a fan dipole, which needs a wire per band and a center support.

## Cookbook lengths

| Half-wave at | Length | Common name |
|--------------|--------|-------------|
| 7.150 MHz (40 m) | 65 ft 5 in | 40 m EFHW (single-band) |
| 3.550 MHz (80 m) | 131 ft 8 in | "80–10" EFHW (5-band harmonic) |
| 1.835 MHz (160 m) | 254 ft 10 in | "160–10" EFHW (very long; needs space) |

For 80–10 multi-band coverage, the most common practical length is **130 to 135 feet**. The exact number depends on ground proximity and wire diameter; tune for best SWR on whichever band is most important to you.

## Counterpoise

EFHWs are often advertised "no counterpoise needed." That's *almost* true:

- The unun's primary side, a few feet of feedline as **common-mode counterpoise**, and the antenna analyzer / shack ground all interact to act as the "missing" ground reference.
- A short wire (typically **0.05 λ at the lowest band**, so ~5 ft for 80 m use) attached to the unun's coax-shield side often improves performance and lowers SWR variability.
- A **common-mode choke** in the feedline 0.05 λ from the unun (about 5 ft on 80 m) prevents shack RF and forces the antenna to use a defined section of feedline as counterpoise rather than the whole shack ground.

Skip both the counterpoise and the choke and you'll have RF in the shack, weird SWR depending on where you stand, and audio reports of distortion.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** The "no-counterpoise" claim works because the EFHW is fed at a current minimum (high voltage / low current point), so the return current that has to flow back into the unun's "ground" is small. But "small" isn't "zero." A proper counterpoise stabilizes the antenna against installation variation. Without one, the feedline shield itself becomes the counterpoise — leading to common-mode currents in the shack. The 0.05 λ counterpoise + choke is an inexpensive insurance policy.

## SWR behavior

A well-designed 80–10 EFHW will typically show:

- **40 m**: SWR < 1.5:1 across the band
- **20 m**: SWR < 1.5:1 across the band
- **15 m**: SWR ~1.7:1
- **10 m**: SWR ~2:1, may need slight tuning
- **80 m**: Bandwidth narrow (~50 kHz under 2:1); use a tuner for full-band coverage

If your SWR is high on every band, the unun is mismatched (wrong ratio for your install) or the wire length is wrong. If SWR is high only on one band, that band's resonance shifted (length problem, environment problem).

## Power handling

The transformer is the bottleneck. Common ratings:

| Toroid | Approx. continuous power |
|--------|--------------------------|
| FT140-43 (single core) | 50–100 W SSB (lower for digital) |
| FT240-43 (single core) | 200–500 W SSB |
| FT240-43 (stacked pair) | 1000 W+ SSB |

**Digital modes (FT8, RTTY) are 100% duty cycle.** Halve the SSB rating for digital. A 100-W FT8 station should use at least an FT240-class unun.

If the unun's box is hot to the touch after a transmission, it's losing power to heat and you should size up.

## Installation patterns

- **Sloper** — unun at one end at low height (4–8 ft), wire ascending to a tree or mast at the far end. The most common practical installation; works very well.
- **Inverted L** — unun at the bottom, wire vertical for the first 30–40 ft, then horizontal. Good DX antenna on 80/40 m if you have one tall support.
- **Horizontal** — unun at one end at the same height as the rest of the wire, going to a far support. Acts more like a long horizontal antenna; works well for high bands, less ideal on 80 m.
- **Attic / portable** — short EFHW (40 m or 20 m) folded along the ridge of an attic or run between trees on a SOTA hike. The EFHW's small unun makes this much more practical than a center-fed dipole.

## Common mistakes

- **Wrong unun ratio.** A 49:1 in an installation that wants 64:1 (or vice versa) gives high SWR you cannot fix with length tuning.
- **No common-mode choke.** Symptoms: SWR depends on what you touch, audio distortion, shack RFI.
- **Cheap unun.** A undersized core saturates at high power and the antenna stops working over a few minutes of TX. Buy quality (Balun Designs, Palomar, KM4ACK kits) or wind your own with FT240-43.
- **Ground-skimming wire.** The far end of the EFHW is *also* a high-voltage point. Keep it 8 ft above ground or higher.
- **Resonance set at low height, then raised.** The EFHW is sensitive to ground proximity; tune at final height.

## When to pick an EFHW

- One support only.
- You want multi-band, no traps, no fan, no tuner-required operation.
- Portable / SOTA / POTA — the form factor wins.
- Attic install — the geometry matches.

## When to avoid

- You want maximum efficiency on 80 m with a narrow-band signal. A full-size dipole still wins.
- You need a balanced antenna for some specific reason (rare).
- You can't tolerate a counterpoise wire near the unun (e.g., extremely tight space).
