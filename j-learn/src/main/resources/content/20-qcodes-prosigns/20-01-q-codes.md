# Q-Codes

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

This is the alphabetical reference for Q-codes that appear in amateur use. Each entry shows the **statement form** (default) and the **question form** (with `?` appended). Most have both; a few are statement-only.

The amateur-relevant subset is small — about 30-40 Q-codes regularly seen. The full ITU Q-code dictionary (covering aeronautical, maritime, and military services) is much larger. This section focuses on what you'll actually encounter as a ham.

## Most-used (learn these first)

These show up in nearly every CW QSO and many SSB/digital-mode conversations.

### QRZ
- **Question**: "Who is calling me?" — what you transmit when you hear someone calling you but don't catch the callsign.
- **Statement**: rare; sometimes "I am being called by..." but usually just heard as the question form.

### QSL
- **Question**: "Can you confirm receipt?" / "Did you receive my last message?"
- **Statement**: "I acknowledge receipt." / "Confirmed."

Beyond the on-air use, **QSL card** has become the noun for the physical (or now electronic) confirmation card amateurs exchange after a contact.

### QSO
- **Question**: "Can you communicate with...?"
- **Statement**: "I can communicate with..."

In modern amateur use, **QSO** is the universal noun for "a contact" — "I had a 20-minute QSO with Australia."

### QRT
- **Question**: "Shall I stop sending?"
- **Statement**: "Stop sending; I am closing down." / "I am going off the air."

Often spoken: "I'm going QRT for dinner." = "I'm leaving the radio."

### QTH
- **Question**: "What is your location?"
- **Statement**: "My location is..." (followed by city/state/grid square).

The **noun** "QTH" means "the operator's location/station." "His QTH is in Maine."

### QRM
- **Question**: "Are you suffering from interference?"
- **Statement**: "I am being interfered with by..." (specifically: man-made interference — other stations, intentional jamming, etc.)

### QRN
- **Question**: "Are you troubled by static?"
- **Statement**: "I am troubled by atmospheric noise." (specifically: natural noise — lightning, ionospheric, etc.)

QRM and QRN distinguish the two types of noise: **QRM is human, QRN is natural**. The distinction matters because the fixes differ — QRM you might tune around or wait out; QRN is propagation/weather.

### QSB
- **Question**: "Is your signal fading?"
- **Statement**: "Your signals are fading." (or "Mine are.")

The QSB phenomenon is signal *amplitude variation*, typically with periods of seconds to minutes — caused by ionospheric multipath. "QSB" as a verb is common: "the signal is QSBing badly."

### QSY
- **Question**: "Shall I change frequency?" — also "Shall I send on a different frequency?"
- **Statement**: "Change to transmission on another frequency." / "I am changing to..."

Used as a verb: "Let's QSY to 14.180." = "Let's move to 14.180."

### QSP
- **Question**: "Will you relay to...?"
- **Statement**: "I will relay to..."

Used in NTS (National Traffic System) and tactical traffic handling: "Can you QSP this message to W1AW?"

### QRS / QRQ
- **QRS** statement: "Send slower." (Question: "Shall I send slower?")
- **QRQ** statement: "Send faster." (Question: "Shall I send faster?")

A new CW operator often sends "PSE QRS" ("please send slower") to get a more proficient operator to slow down to a copyable speed.

### QRP / QRO
- **QRP** statement: "Decrease power." / "I am running low power." (Now used as a noun: "QRP operation" = low-power operation, typically 5W or less.)
- **QRO** statement: "Increase power." / "I am running high power."

QRP and QRO have become identifiers for two amateur sub-cultures — QRP enthusiasts deliberately run minimal power for the challenge; QRO operators run amplifiers near the legal limit.

## Operating-procedure codes

Less common but useful.

### QRA
- **Question**: "What is the name of your station?" (in maritime use, the ship's identity).
- **Statement**: "My station is..."

In amateur context, sometimes used to mean callsign or operator name; rare in modern usage.

### QRG
- **Question**: "What is my exact frequency?" / "Will you tell me my exact frequency?"
- **Statement**: "Your exact frequency is..."

Used when you want a far-end station to confirm your transmit frequency.

### QRH
- **Question**: "Does my frequency vary?"
- **Statement**: "Your frequency varies." (= you're drifting or chirping.)

Useful for diagnosing transmitter problems on the other end.

### QRI
- **Question**: "How is the tone of my transmission?"
- **Statement**: "The tone of your transmission is..." (rated 1-3, with 1 = good DC tone, 3 = bad/chirpy).

Largely historical — modern transmitters all have clean tones.

### QRK
- **Question**: "What is the readability of my signal?"
- **Statement**: "The readability of your signal is..." (rated 1-5, with 5 = perfectly readable, 1 = unreadable).

The "R" in the RST signal report (Readability-Strength-Tone). RST 599 means readability 5, strength 9, tone 9.

### QRL
- **Question**: "Are you busy?" / "Is the frequency busy?"
- **Statement**: "I am busy." / "The frequency is in use."

Sent at the start of any operation on a new frequency to make sure the frequency isn't already in use. Standard practice: "QRL?" — wait — if no answer, the frequency is yours.

### QRU
- **Question**: "Do you have anything for me?"
- **Statement**: "I have nothing for you."

Used in traffic handling to ask if there are any messages.

### QRV
- **Question**: "Are you ready?"
- **Statement**: "I am ready."

Often "QRV?" before starting a sked or contest exchange.

### QRX
- **Question**: "When will you call again?" / "Will you wait?"
- **Statement**: "I will call again at..." / "Stand by." (followed by a time or "minutes").

"QRX 5" = "stand by 5 minutes."

### QSA
- **Question**: "What is the strength of my signal?"
- **Statement**: "The strength of your signal is..." (rated 1-5).

The "S" in RST. Less specific than the more common 1-9 S-meter scale.

### QSK
- **Question**: "Can you hear between your signals?" / "Can you receive while transmitting?"
- **Statement**: "I can hear between my signals." (= I have full break-in QSK CW).

Modern transceivers with QSK can hear during the gaps between dots and dashes. Knowing the other op has QSK lets you "break" mid-transmission.

### QSV
- **Question**: "Shall I send a series of V's?"
- **Statement**: "Send a series of V's." (Used for tuning at the receiving end.)

Sending "VVV VVV VVV" lets the far-end station tune their receiver to your signal.

### QTC
- **Question**: "How many messages have you to send?"
- **Statement**: "I have ... messages for you." (Followed by a count.)

Used in contests with traffic exchange (HF or in NTS): "QTC 5" = "I have 5 messages."

### QTR
- **Question**: "What is the exact time?"
- **Statement**: "The time is..."

Used for time synchronization in nets or time checks.

## Procedural / less common

### QSG
- "Shall I send ... messages at a time?" — used in heavy traffic handling.

### QSU
- "Shall I send or reply on this frequency?"

### QSW
- "Will you send on this frequency?" — used to confirm someone will answer on a specific frequency.

### QSX
- **Question**: "Will you listen on ... kHz?"
- **Statement**: "I am listening on ..."

Heard frequently in DX QSO call-signs: "VK4ABC up 5" effectively means "I am QSX 14.020 + 5 kHz" — the DX station is listening on a different frequency than they're transmitting on (split operation).

### QSZ
- "Shall I send each word twice?" — for repeats in poor conditions.

### QTV
- "Shall I stand guard for you on ... kHz?" — handing off a watch frequency.

### QTX
- "Will you keep your station open for further communication with me until ...?"

## Amateur-specific compounds

These aren't strict ITU Q-codes but appear constantly in amateur use:

### "RR73" / "TU 73"
"RR" = "I confirm" (from R or "Roger"). "TU" = "Thank you." Followed by 73 ("best regards").

### "73 ES GL"
"73 and good luck." Common close-out. The "ES" is "and" (from CW where it's an alternative to spelling out "and").

### "CUL 73"
"See you later, 73." Even more casual close-out.

### "QSL VIA..."
Common in DX-pedition info: "QSL VIA M0OXO" = "Send your QSL card to M0OXO who is acting as the QSL manager."

## Q-codes that mean different things in different services

Some Q-codes have different meanings in maritime, aeronautical, military, and amateur services. The amateur use is what's listed above. If you're decoding maritime or aeronautical traffic, consult a service-specific Q-code dictionary.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** The original ITU Q-code allocation reserves QAA-QNZ for aeronautical use, QOA-QQZ for maritime, and QRA-QUZ for general (which amateurs share). Within those ranges, individual codes may have parallel meanings — QSA in maritime use is signal-strength (same as amateur), but QHE in aeronautical use is "all clear" while QHE has no amateur use. The codes that exist in multiple services tend to be the most universally understood.

## How to remember them

A few mnemonics that work for some operators:

- **QRZ** — "who's calling me?" (Z for "who?" sounds like "qui?")
- **QSL** — "I confirm receipt" (L for "letter" you'll send to confirm)
- **QRM/QRN** — M is "made" (man-made), N is "nature" (natural)
- **QRT** — T is "terminate" (stop sending)
- **QTH** — TH is "Town" "Here" (location)

These are post-hoc; the original codes weren't designed with these meanings, but they help retention.
