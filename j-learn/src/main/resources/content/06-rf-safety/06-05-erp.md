# ERP

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

**Effective Radiated Power (ERP)** is your antenna system's "as-radiated" output — the transmitter's power, minus feedline and matching losses, multiplied by antenna gain in the relevant direction. For RF safety calculations, ERP (or its sibling EIRP) is what determines the field strength at any given distance from the antenna.

This section explains the difference between ERP and EIRP, how to compute either from your station's components, and why this number — not just transmitter wattage — is what RF exposure calculators care about.

A related concept appears in §09 (Power Budget); this section is the RF-safety-specific take. The two are consistent and should be read together.

## ERP vs. EIRP

Two slightly different quantities:

- **ERP (Effective Radiated Power)** = transmitter power × antenna gain *over a half-wave dipole* (i.e., gain in dBd).
- **EIRP (Equivalent Isotropic Radiated Power)** = transmitter power × antenna gain *over an isotropic radiator* (i.e., gain in dBi).

**EIRP = ERP × 1.64** (because a dipole is 2.15 dBi over isotropic = 1.64× linear).

Or equivalently: **EIRP_dBW = ERP_dBW + 2.15 dB**.

The FCC uses **EIRP** in 47 CFR 1.1310 for power-density calculations. Some older amateur references (especially the pre-2021 ARRL Antenna Book) use ERP. **Be careful when reading specs — know which one is meant.**

For RF safety calculations in the United States, **use EIRP** (the FCC standard).

## Computing EIRP

EIRP = TX power × (1 / feedline loss factor) × (1 / matching network loss factor) × (antenna gain in linear ratio)

In dB form:

**EIRP (dBW) = TX power (dBW) − feedline loss (dB) − matching loss (dB) + antenna gain (dBi)**

Where 1 W = 0 dBW; 100 W = 20 dBW; 1500 W = 31.76 dBW.

### Example calculations

**Station 1**: 100 W TX, 1.5 dB feedline loss, no separate matching loss, 5 dBi gain dipole.

EIRP = 20 dBW − 1.5 + 5 = 23.5 dBW = 224 W.

**Station 2**: 1500 W TX, 0.5 dB feedline loss, 0.3 dB tuner loss, 8 dBi gain triband Yagi.

EIRP = 31.76 dBW − 0.5 − 0.3 + 8 = 38.96 dBW = 7870 W.

**Station 3**: 5 W HT, 0 dB feedline (built-in antenna), 2 dBi rubber duck.

EIRP = 7 dBW + 2 = 9 dBW = 7.94 W.

### What EIRP tells you

EIRP × radiation pattern shape = **field strength at every direction and distance**. For RF safety, what we actually evaluate is:

**Power density (mW/cm²) = EIRP (W) / (4π × d² (cm²))**

In far-field conditions. Practical form for amateur use:

**Power density (mW/cm²) = 0.0796 × EIRP (W) / d² (m²)**

Or with d in feet:

**Power density (mW/cm²) = 0.000064 × EIRP(W) / d²(ft)**

(Note: this matches the standard formula in §06-02. Both are equivalent.)

## What "antenna gain" means in this context

Antenna gain is direction-dependent. The relevant gain for RF exposure calculations is **the gain in the direction of the location being evaluated**, not the antenna's peak gain.

For a vertical antenna (omnidirectional in azimuth), the relevant gain is the antenna's gain at the elevation angle from the antenna to the bystander location. For a person standing at ground level 30 ft from a 25-ft-high vertical, the elevation angle from antenna to bystander is small but downward — and the vertical's gain at that angle (below the horizon) may be 5–10 dB *less* than its peak gain. Using peak gain in this case would overestimate the exposure.

For a Yagi pointed at the DX path, the gain in the broadside direction (sidelobe area, where the neighbor's house is) might be 15 dB lower than the forward gain. Using forward gain to evaluate the neighbor would massively overestimate exposure.

**For conservative compliance**: use the antenna's peak gain in the direction of the bystander, considering azimuth and elevation. Most amateur evaluators just use the peak gain (worst-case) and accept the conservative result.

## The "feedline gain" trap

If your feedline is lossy enough to matter, you're losing transmit power as heat in the coax. This is **subtractive in EIRP** (good for safety) and **subtractive in DX performance** (bad for actual operating).

A 100-ft RG-58 run at 144 MHz loses about 6.5 dB. A 100 W transmitter into 0 dB gain antenna with this feedline produces:

EIRP = 20 dBW − 6.5 + 0 = 13.5 dBW = 22 W

Your "100 W station" is, from the bystander's perspective, a 22 W station. This is one reason (sometimes the only reason) high-loss feedline accidentally creates compliance margin you weren't trying for.

This is also why **low-loss feedline (LMR-400, hardline) doesn't always make the operator's life easier** — better feedline means more EIRP, which can put the station closer to MPE limits at distant bystander locations.

## ERP: equivalent calculation in different units

Some references use:

- **Power gain expressed as a numeric ratio** (e.g., "antenna gain = 4×" for a 6 dBd antenna)
- **Loss expressed as a numeric ratio** (e.g., "feedline loss = 0.7×" for a 1.5 dB loss)

Then EIRP_W = TX_W × (1 / 0.7) × 4 × 1.64 (the 1.64 converts dBd-style gain to dBi-style for EIRP).

The dB form is usually easier to manipulate.

## When the calculation gets harder

Three scenarios where the simple "TX × gain / feedline" calculation breaks:

1. **Multi-band antennas**: each band has different gain and feedline loss. Compute EIRP separately for each band; evaluate MPE separately for each band.
2. **Tuners and matching networks with significant loss**: a tuner matching a high-SWR antenna may lose 1–3 dB. This subtracts from your EIRP. Account for it.
3. **Phased arrays, multi-feed antennas**: total radiated power equals the sum of the per-feed powers; the gain is the array's total directional gain. Use modeling.

## Practical EIRP numbers worth memorizing

For a 100 W SSB station (typical entry-level):

| Antenna | Approx. gain | Approx. EIRP |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| Dipole at 0.5 λ over good ground | 6.5 dBi | 450 W |
| Vertical with good radials | 1 dBi (at peak elevation) | 130 W |
| 3-element Yagi at 1 λ | 11 dBi | 1300 W |
| 4-element Yagi at 1 λ | 12 dBi | 1600 W |

For a 1500 W SSB station:

| Antenna | EIRP |
|---------|------|
| Dipole at 0.5 λ | 6.7 kW |
| 3-element Yagi at 1 λ | 19 kW |
| 5-element Yagi at 1.5 λ at 14 MHz | 24 kW |

This is why high-power Yagi stations are the case where MPE evaluations get tight. EIRP can exceed 20 kW; the field at the neighbor's deck can exceed 0.92 mW/cm² (uncontrolled MPE limit at 14 MHz) at 30 ft if the beam is pointed there.

## EIRP and the bystander's experience

A useful intuition: **at 100 m from a 1 kW EIRP source, the field is the same as at the distance from a 100 W bulb (sound) at 1 m** — both produce similar relative apparent intensities of their respective energy types at those distances. (This is a hand-waving comparison, not a real physics relation.) The more precise statement: power density falls as 1/distance², so doubling distance reduces field by 6 dB.

Practical rule: **if your station's EIRP is X watts, a non-compliant location at distance d becomes compliant at distance d × √(X / X_compliant)**. So a 4× EIRP increase requires a 2× distance increase to maintain the same field strength.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** EIRP is fundamentally a **far-field** concept; it presumes the antenna is being viewed from far enough away that the radiation pattern can be characterized by a single gain number per direction. In the near field, EIRP doesn't directly translate to field strength; near-field intensities can be much higher than the EIRP-based far-field formula predicts. For evaluation of bystander locations within ~2D²/λ of the antenna (D = largest antenna dimension), use NEC near-field calculation or apply a 2-3× upward correction factor to the EIRP-based estimate.

## Common mistakes

- **Using TX wattage instead of EIRP.** Most online calculators want EIRP; if you give them transmitter wattage, they assume gain = 0 dBi (isotropic) and the calculation underestimates exposure for any directional antenna.
- **Forgetting feedline loss.** Subtract it.
- **Using peak gain when the bystander is in a sidelobe.** Conservative but pessimistic; the real exposure may be 10+ dB lower in the sidelobe.
- **Mixing up dBd and dBi.** dBi = dBd + 2.15. Calculators specify which they want.
- **Ignoring tuner loss.** A 1500 W transmitter into a tuner with 1 dB loss has 1190 W after the tuner, not 1500 W.
