# Operating Practice — Overview

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

This chapter is the etiquette manual for everyday on-air operating — the customs and procedures that aren't legally mandated but make amateur radio actually work as a shared resource. They're the difference between the operator everyone wants to QSO and the one nobody calls back.

This is **not** the regulations chapter. The FCC rules (§97) tell you what's legal; this chapter tells you what's good practice. Most rules-of-the-road in amateur radio are conventions, not laws — they evolve through community use and become standards because they make the bands functional.

## The big idea

Amateur frequencies are shared. Every transmission you make happens **in the same physical space** as everyone else's. Operating practice exists because shared resources only work when participants follow conventions — usually informal, sometimes regulatory, almost always for a reason.

A few principles run through this chapter:

- **Listen before transmitting.** Whatever you'd say into the mic, the band probably has a better answer if you listen for 30 seconds first.
- **Use the minimum power necessary** (§97.313). It's a regulation, but it's also good etiquette: 100 W gets you to your contact; 1500 W gets you to your contact AND wipes out three other QSOs the next county over.
- **Keep transmissions short.** Brevity is bandwidth. Long transmissions block other operators; short ones share the resource.
- **Identify when required.** Every 10 minutes during a QSO; at the end. Tactical operating still needs FCC ID.
- **Don't be the noise.** Tune into a dummy load, not on top of someone else's contact. Don't intentionally cause interference (§97.101).
- **Pile-ups have rules.** Working a rare DX station is a learnable skill — calling once and listening, not transmitting on top of the DX, recognizing when you're being picked.

## Who this chapter is for

- **New operators** building their on-air habits. Get these right early; they're hard to unlearn later.
- **Returning operators** who have been off the air long enough that the conventions have drifted (FT8 etiquette didn't exist 20 years ago).
- **Operators expanding into new modes** — the conventions for HF SSB are different from FM repeater operating, which is different from CW DXing, which is different from satellite operating.

## A note on tone

Some of this material can read as fussy. It isn't. The bands are crowded; small courtesies are how a thousand operators share 50 kHz of spectrum at the same time. An operator who can't be bothered to ask "is the frequency in use?" is the operator everyone else avoids working — and that's a self-inflicted wound for them, not for the community.

The good news: these aren't hard. Most of operating practice is just paying attention, and the rest is muscle memory after a hundred QSOs.
