# Koch Method

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

The **Koch method** is the way to learn Morse code that actually works. It was developed by Ludwig Koch in Germany in the 1930s, validated by decades of military and commercial training, and is the basis of every modern CW course (CWops, LCWO, G4FON). If anyone teaches you a different method — a chart of dots and dashes, "memorize this picture of the alphabet," or "use a mnemonic for each letter" — politely ignore them. None of those scale.

## The two key insights

Koch's method is built on two findings:

1. **Learn at the target speed from day one.** Most adults instinctively want to start slow and speed up. This produces operators who hear individual *dits* and *dahs* and translate them mentally into letters. That mental translation step is a wall you hit at about 12 WPM and never get past. Koch eliminates it by training the brain to recognize the *sound of a whole letter at full speed* from the very first character.

2. **Add one character at a time.** Don't try to learn the alphabet at once. Master two characters first; when you can copy them at 90% accuracy, add the third. When you can copy three, add the fourth. And so on, until all 40 characters (26 letters + 10 digits + ., , / ?) are in your repertoire.

This sounds slow but is actually fast — most students cover all 40 characters in 6–10 weeks of daily 30-minute sessions.

## What "target speed" means

You pick a target character speed and stick with it. The standard target is **20 WPM character speed** (sometimes called 25 WPM character speed for ambitious students). At that rate, individual characters are sent at the speed you want to ultimately copy them.

To make beginners' lives bearable, the **spacing between characters and words is stretched** — this is **Farnsworth spacing** (covered in §05-02). The character "K" still sounds like a 20 WPM K, but there's a long gap before the next character arrives. As you improve, you reduce the gap. The character speed never drops.

## The exact procedure

Day 1:
1. Set your trainer (LCWO is fine) to **20 WPM character speed**, **5 WPM effective speed** (lots of inter-character gap).
2. Choose two characters: **K** and **M**. (Koch's original order — see §05-03.)
3. Run the trainer for **5 minutes**. It plays K's and M's randomly. You write down what you hear.
4. Check your accuracy. Below 90%? Run another 5 minutes.
5. Repeat for **30 minutes total** if you can stand it. Stop when you stop improving.

Day 2:
- Same two characters until you reliably hit 90%+. Most people get there in 1–2 sessions.

Day N (when you hit 90%):
- Add the next character (Koch's order: K M U R E S N A P T L W I . J Z = F O Y , V G 5 / Q 9 2 H 3 8 B ? 4 7 C 1 D 6 0 X).
- Run sessions with **all characters learned so far** mixed randomly.
- Don't repeat lessons. Once you have a character at 90%, it stays in the rotation forever.

## Why "K M" first?

Koch picked the starting characters for **maximum sound contrast**:

- **K** = `–·–` (dah-dit-dah)
- **M** = `– –` (dah-dah)

These two are unmistakably different. You can tell them apart without effort, which lets you focus on the *sound of a 20 WPM character* without also struggling to distinguish similar shapes. By the time the trainer adds U (`··–`), your brain has already locked in the rhythm.

Some modern courses (including LCWO's default) use a slightly different order optimized for early word-formation. Whichever you pick, **stick with one order**. Don't switch trainers mid-stream.

## What's hard about it

The first three weeks. Specifically:

- **You feel stupid for the first week.** The trainer plays a character; your brain freezes; you write down nothing. This is normal. The brain is building new auditory recognition pathways and they don't form overnight.
- **You'll plateau on character 3 or 4.** Adding the third character feels like a small change but suddenly demands you discriminate between three sounds in real time. This is the wall most adults quit at. **Push through.** A week of daily sessions and you're past it.
- **The first time you copy a real callsign you don't know is a religious experience.** Until then it's all random characters. Then one day the trainer sends `WM3J` and you read it as a callsign instead of four characters. You're past the worst.

## What's easy about it

After about character 8:

- **New characters get faster to learn.** Where character 3 took 3 days, character 12 takes one session.
- **You start hearing words.** When you've got E S T A N I O R H — the most common letters in English — your brain spontaneously recognizes simple words.
- **Speed is just practice.** Once all 40 characters are in your head at 20 WPM character speed, raising the *effective* speed (closing the inter-character gap) is just hours of practice.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | What to do instead |
|---------|--------------|---------------------|
| Starting at 5 WPM character speed | Can't transition to higher speeds later | Start at 20 WPM character speed from day one |
| Memorizing dots and dashes | Forces translation step | Recognize the sound of the whole character |
| Skipping characters that are "hard" | They don't get easier | Drill them more, don't skip |
| Learning sending first | Sending without copying produces bad habits | Copy first, then send |
| 2 hours on Saturday and nothing all week | Not enough repetition | 30 min/day every day beats 2 hours once |
| Using mnemonics ("dah-di-dah is K, like Kit") | They become the wall at 12 WPM | Trust the Koch method |

## How long until I can have a QSO?

Rough timeline for typical adult learner doing 30 minutes daily:

| Time | Where you are |
|------|---------------|
| Week 1 | 4–5 characters |
| Week 3 | 12–15 characters |
| Week 6 | All 40 characters at 5–8 WPM effective speed |
| Week 10 | 12–15 WPM effective; can hold a slow QSO |
| Week 14 | 18–20 WPM; comfortable in pile-ups |
| Month 6+ | 25+ WPM if you keep at it |

Most CWops academy students reach 25 WPM in their second 8-week semester. Plenty of self-taught operators reach the same speed without the formal class.

## What to copy

Don't just train against random characters forever. Once you have all the alphabet, mix in:

- **Real QSO recordings** — there are hundreds on YouTube under "morse code QSO practice."
- **W1AW code practice broadcasts** — ARRL transmits practice text on multiple bands daily, at speeds from 5 to 35 WPM.
- **Live on-air listening** — tune to the CW segments of any band and listen. Even copying part of a signal is good practice.
- **CW Academy lessons** — structured QSO scripts of increasing complexity.
