# Send Practice

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

Copying CW (receiving) and sending CW are different skills. You can be a perfect copyist and send miserably; you can be a great sender and miss half of what comes back. Most operators learn copy first, then sending — but some experienced ops will tell you to start sending sooner. The truth is that they reinforce each other, so once you've got 5 or 6 characters down in copy, start practicing sending too.

## Three kinds of keys

### Straight key

The classic up-down lever. You produce dits and dahs by holding the key down for short and long times respectively. **You** control all the timing.

- **Pros:** Simple. Cheap. Romantic. Forces you to learn timing.
- **Cons:** Hard to maintain timing at speeds above 18–20 WPM. Hand fatigue is real on long sessions.
- **Best for:** Learning fundamentals, slow QSOs (5–15 WPM), SKCC operators (a club dedicated to straight-key operating), portable use where simplicity matters.

A good cheap straight key: **Speed-X J-38** (military surplus, ~$30 used) or any decent ham-shop new key (~$50–80 new).

### Iambic paddle (also called "single lever" or "dual lever" paddle)

Two horizontal paddles. Squeezing the dit paddle sends a dit; squeezing the dah paddle sends a dah. Squeezing **both at once** sends an alternating pattern of dits and dahs (the "iambic" mode). The radio's electronic keyer handles all timing — you just tap the paddles.

- **Pros:** Trivial timing. Comfortable for long sessions. Clean, machine-perfect output. Scales to high speeds (35+ WPM with practice).
- **Cons:** More expensive. Requires an electronic keyer (built into every modern rig). Slightly more abstract — you're tapping a switch, not "making" the dits.
- **Best for:** Modern CW operating, contest, anything above 18 WPM.

Recommended starter paddle: **Bencher BY-1/BY-2** (the de-facto US standard, ~$120 new), **Vibroplex Code Warrior**, or any of the dozens of clones (~$50–80).

### Bug (semi-automatic key)

A mechanical sideswiper from the 1900s. Pressing one direction sends a continuous string of dits (mechanically generated by a vibrating arm); the other direction sends manual dahs.

- **Pros:** Distinctive sound — bug operators are recognizable on the air. Historically important.
- **Cons:** Requires significant practice to send well. Anachronistic.
- **Best for:** Vintage operators, niche enthusiasts. Not recommended for beginners.

Famous brands: **Vibroplex Original** (still made; the most iconic).

## Iambic A vs Iambic B

When you have a paddle and squeeze both contacts at once, the keyer alternates dits and dahs. There are two slightly different rules for what happens when you *release* both paddles:

- **Iambic A**: The keyer finishes the current element and stops.
- **Iambic B**: The keyer finishes the current element AND sends one more of the opposite element. Useful for sending characters like K (`–·–`) by tapping both paddles momentarily.

Most US-trained operators use Iambic B. It's slightly faster for some characters once you internalize it. Set the keyer in your radio to whichever you prefer; the difference is small.

## Setting up the paddle and keyer

| Setting | Where it lives | Reasonable starting value |
|---------|----------------|---------------------------|
| Keyer mode | Radio menu | Iambic A or Iambic B |
| Speed | Radio front panel knob | 18 WPM (set to your comfortable receive speed) |
| Weight | Radio menu | 50% (dah/dit ratio = 3:1, the standard) |
| Sidetone | Radio menu | 600–700 Hz, comfortable volume |
| Paddle reverse | Radio menu | Off (left = dit, right = dah for right-handed) |

Adjust the paddle's mechanical contact spacing and tension to your preference. Most paddles ship with the gap set wide and the tension set heavy; tightening up the gap (so contacts close after very small finger movement) and reducing the tension makes for less hand fatigue.

## How to send a clean character

The hard part isn't speed — it's **clean spacing**. Listen to a great CW operator and you'll notice:

- **Dits and dahs of consistent length** — no wobble between fast dits and slow ones.
- **Even gaps within characters** — the gap inside an "S" (`···`) is the same as the gap inside an "H" (`····`).
- **Consistent gaps between characters** — three dit-lengths between every character, no more, no less.
- **Word gaps clearly longer than character gaps** — so you can hear the breaks.

With a paddle and keyer, all the within-character timing is automatic. You only have to control the inter-character timing, which is the easy part.

With a straight key, you have to control all of it. Your sending will sound rough at first. **Practice with a metronome** — at slow speeds, send a string of "VVV" with the metronome ticking each dit. Then add dahs. Then characters. Then words. Discipline pays off.

## What to send

Practice scripts for sending:

```
Beginner (5 minutes/day, week 1):
   K M K M K M K M K M K M

Day 2:
   KM MK KM KM MK MK KM
   MMM KKK MMK KKM

Week 2 (with U R):
   KMUR UMRK RKMU MURK
   USE RUM RUSE MUR RUM RUSE

Week 3 (with E S N A P T):
   THE AND USE ARE PUT TEN PASS
   PRE-PASS PASTA NEAR PEN
```

Send a test piece, **listen back** to your own sidetone, and grade yourself:

- Was each dit the same length?
- Was each dah ~3 dits long?
- Were the gaps even?
- Did you stutter or pause oddly?

Self-listening is by far the fastest way to improve sending.

## Recording your fist

Your "fist" is your distinctive sending style. Even with a perfect electronic keyer, individual operators have recognizable rhythms — pauses where they think, the specific way they handle word gaps, occasional flourishes.

Record a few minutes of your sending periodically. Listen back a week later. You'll hear yourself with fresh ears and notice habits you didn't realize you had.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** A "good fist" historically meant clean, even straight-key sending. The phrase predates electronic keyers. Today the term still applies — operators sending with a paddle and keyer have a fist defined by their inter-character timing, their handling of common abbreviations (do you send "73" with a single inter-character gap or two?), and their use of ad-libbed words. CW skimmers can identify operators by their fist with surprising accuracy after a few minutes of listening, which is one reason competitive contest ops vary their inter-character timing slightly to remain "anonymous" between different stations.

## Common sending mistakes

| Mistake | Symptom | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Inconsistent dit length (straight key) | Wavering, "drunken" sound | Use a metronome; slow down; record and listen |
| Too short word gaps | Words run together; copyist asks for repeats | Count to seven dits between words |
| "Swing" — uneven dah lengths | Sounds like jazz, not CW | Slow down; with paddle, check keyer weight setting |
| Heavy first dit/dah | Each character starts with extra long element | Loosen finger pressure on the paddle |
| Overshoot at speed | "Ks" come out as "Cs" because you held both paddles a beat too long | Practice the squeeze-release timing slowly |

## Software send-trainers

Several apps grade your sending:

- **MorseToyz** — captures sidetone via mic and analyzes timing.
- **Morse Code Decoder** apps on phones — point at your radio, see what you actually sent vs what you meant to send.
- **Ham radio CW decoders** in your radio (some Yaesu/Icom rigs have them) — useful for self-checking.

These won't replace listening to your own sending, but they can catch problems you've stopped noticing.

## When to start sending on the air

When you can:

- Send the alphabet cleanly at your target receive speed.
- Send your own callsign without thinking about it.
- Send a short canned phrase ("CQ DE WM3J K") without errors.
- Self-correct if you make a mistake (most operators send `..........` — eight dits — to mean "I made an error, let me re-send the word").

Your first on-air sending will be your callsign in answer to someone's CQ. That's a 4–6 character transmission. Even if it goes badly, the other operator will be charitable. Just send it clean and go.
