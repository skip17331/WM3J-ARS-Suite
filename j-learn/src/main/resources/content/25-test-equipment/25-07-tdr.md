# TDR — Time-Domain Reflectometry

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

A **TDR** (time-domain reflectometer) injects a fast pulse into one end of a cable and measures the **reflections** that come back from anything inside the cable that isn't 50 Ω. By measuring how long each reflection takes to return and how big it is, you locate cable faults with surprising precision — often within inches over hundreds of feet.

Two amateur-relevant facts:

1. **You probably already own one.** A NanoVNA in time-domain mode is a real TDR, no extra gear required (§25-01).
2. **It's the fastest way to find buried cable damage.** A waterlogged buried feedline that reads "high SWR" to a power meter shows up as a clear reflection at 47 ft on a TDR.

## How it works

The TDR sends a step (an abrupt voltage rise) into the cable's input. The step travels down the cable at the cable's velocity factor — typically 0.66c for solid polyethylene coax, 0.85c for foam. When it encounters an impedance discontinuity, **part of the energy reflects back** with an amplitude proportional to the mismatch:

```
Reflection coefficient ρ = (Z_fault - Z_line) / (Z_fault + Z_line)
```

For a 50 Ω line:

- **Open circuit** (Z = ∞): ρ = +1.0 (full reflection, same polarity as incident step)
- **Short circuit** (Z = 0): ρ = -1.0 (full reflection, inverted polarity)
- **75 Ω splice** in a 50 Ω line: ρ = +0.20 (small positive bump)
- **25 Ω water short**: ρ = -0.33 (small negative dip)
- **Perfectly matched termination**: ρ = 0 (no reflection at all)

The TDR receives the reflection some time `t` later. The cable's round-trip distance is `t × c × VF`. One-way distance is half that.

## What you see on the display

A TDR display is essentially **"reflection amplitude vs. distance"** — distance computed from time using the VF. A clean cable into an open looks like:

```
   amplitude
      │
      │                              ┌────────  open at far end
      │                              │
   ρ=0│────────────────────────────  │
      │                              │
      │                              │
      └──────────────────────────────────────► distance (m)
      0                              100 m
```

A cable with a partial reflection at 47 m (say, water intrusion at a buried splice):

```
   amplitude
      │
      │           dip                  ┌────  far-end open
      │   ──────────╲╱─────────────────│
   ρ=0│──────────────╲                 │
      │               ╲────────────────│
      │                                │
      └──────────────────────────────────────► distance
                ▲                      ▲
                47 m: water short       far end
```

The shape of the reflection tells you what kind of fault:

| Pattern | Fault type |
|---------|-----------|
| Sharp positive step | Open (cable cut, connector unscrewed) |
| Sharp negative step | Short (water bridging center conductor to shield) |
| Small positive bump returning to baseline | Connector mismatch, splice with wrong impedance |
| Small negative dip returning to baseline | Water-impregnated section, crushed cable |
| Gradual rise with no clear feature | Distributed loss (cable degrading along length) |
| Repeated small reflections | Multiple connectors / barrels — normal for long runs |

## Locating faults to within inches

The distance resolution of a TDR is set by the pulse rise time (or, for FFT-based TDRs like the NanoVNA, by the frequency span). Rule of thumb:

```
Δx ≈ (rise_time × c × VF) / 2
```

For a 10 ns rise time and VF = 0.66, Δx ≈ 1 m. For 100 ps rise time, Δx ≈ 10 mm. Real instruments:

- **NanoVNA** with 0–900 MHz span: resolution ~11 cm (4 inches)
- **NanoVNA-V2 / SAA-2** with 0–3 GHz span: ~3 cm
- **Tektronix 1502** (classic ham/telephony TDR): ~10 cm
- **Keysight N1055A**: ~1 mm

For a typical ham use case ("where is the bad spot in this 200 ft buried run of LMR-400?"), a NanoVNA at ~10 cm resolution is more than adequate. You measure the fault distance, walk 47 m along the buried path, dig the foot of dirt above it, find the bad spot.

## Workflow — finding a cable fault

You suspect your buried feedline is bad. SWR has been creeping up over months. Here's the procedure:

1. **Disconnect both ends of the cable.** Rig end and antenna end. Bring one end to your bench (preferably the rig end, which is usually accessible).
2. **Connect that end to the NanoVNA / TDR.**
3. **Leave the far end OPEN.** Don't terminate, don't connect anything. You want to see the far-end open as a clear reference reflection.
4. **SOL-calibrate** the NanoVNA at the connector you'll use.
5. **Set frequency range** wide — 0 to 900 MHz typical, or whatever your TDR mode supports.
6. **Switch to time-domain mode** (NanoVNA: DISPLAY → TRANSFORM → ON, with "low-pass step" mode).
7. **Read the trace.** You'll see:
   - A small bump at 0 — the connector / SMA reference plane
   - Possibly small bumps along the way — intermediate connectors / barrels
   - A clear step up at the far end — the open termination
   - **Any other significant feature is your fault**

8. **Read the distance** to the fault from the trace's distance axis. Some software (NanoVNA-Saver) lets you place a marker and read distance directly in feet/meters.
9. **Walk to that distance** along the cable route. Dig / inspect. The fault is usually obvious: a chewed-through outer jacket, a cracked connector body filled with water, a buried barrel that wasn't weatherproofed.

## Reading the polarity for the fault type

The **direction** of the reflection step is the most useful diagnostic:

- **Positive step (cable goes high)**: the fault is **high impedance**. Either an open, a partial open (loose connector), or a series fault (broken center conductor with intact shield).
- **Negative step (cable goes low)**: the fault is **low impedance**. Water short, crushed cable, shield-to-center short.
- **Positive bump that returns to baseline**: a localized **mismatch** that the wave passes through. Examples: a barrel connector with the wrong impedance, a 75 Ω splice, a kink that locally raises Z.
- **Negative dip that returns to baseline**: a localized **low-Z section**. Water in a section of cable (water is roughly 80 ε_r, drastically slowing the wave and dropping Z).

Most amateur cable faults are **either water (negative dip) or open (positive step)**. Shorts to the shield are rare in coax because the shield is grounded and the center is well-protected; they happen on damaged hardline / heliax where the jacket has been compromised.

## Setting the velocity factor right

The TDR can only report distance accurately if it knows the cable's VF. Common values:

| Cable | VF |
|-------|-----|
| RG-58/U solid PE | 0.66 |
| RG-58/U foam PE | 0.79 |
| RG-8/U solid PE | 0.66 |
| LMR-240, LMR-400 | 0.83–0.85 |
| Heliax LDF4-50A | 0.88 |
| Window line, 450 Ω ladder line | 0.91–0.95 |
| Twin-lead | 0.82 |

Wrong VF gives proportionally wrong distance. If your TDR says 47 m but you measure 50 m by walking — your VF setting was 6% off. You can calibrate VF empirically by terminating a known-length section in an open and adjusting VF until the far-end reflection lands at the right distance (see §25-01 for the procedure).

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** VF varies with frequency in cables with foam dielectric (slightly), and very strongly in window line / ladder line (whose effective VF depends on wire spacing relative to wavelength). For broad-band TDR work the VF is a single number that's a compromise. For very precise distance measurements use a single-frequency-band TDR sweep matched to the operating frequency.

## NanoVNA in time-domain mode — practical notes

The NanoVNA's time-domain transform is a software FFT-inverse of the frequency-domain S11 sweep. Two modes:

- **Low-pass step.** Best for fault hunting. Looks like a real TDR's step response. Shows opens as steps up, shorts as steps down.
- **Low-pass impulse.** Pulse response — narrower features but harder to read.
- **Bandpass impulse.** Used when the cable can't pass DC (e.g., through a balun). Less common.

For amateur cable hunting, use **low-pass step**. Set the frequency range as wide as your NanoVNA supports (0.05 MHz to 900 MHz or 1500 MHz) for best resolution. Set the time/distance scale to span 1.5× your cable's actual length so the far-end reflection appears clearly in the trace.

NanoVNA-Saver software gives a larger, more readable display than the NanoVNA's small built-in screen, and supports saving traces for later comparison.

## What NOT to do

- **Don't TDR a cable connected to a transmitter.** The TX puts hundreds of volts on the line if keyed; the TDR's input has ~5 V max rating. Always disconnect both ends.
- **Don't TDR through a tuner or balun.** Tuners and baluns aren't impedance-matched in the same way as straight coax — they show as huge reflections that mask everything past them.
- **Don't trust the absolute amplitude.** Calibrated TDRs report ρ accurately; cheap NanoVNA-based TDRs are accurate in *location* but less so in *amplitude*. For fault hunting location is what matters.
- **Don't ignore the near-end reflection.** The connector at your test point produces a small reflection. Calibration removes it from the trace, but if you didn't calibrate, that small bump at 0 m is the SMA, not a fault.

## Common faults found by TDR

Real-world TDR finds in ham stations:

- **Squirrel damage** — chewed outer jacket on RG-8X at 23 ft from feedline entry. Water intrusion caused 4:1 SWR.
- **Loose PL-259** at the antenna base on a 40 ft tower. Cable looked fine; TDR showed a partial open at exactly 40 ft.
- **Forgotten barrel connector** buried in a foam-insulator splice. Water absorption into the splice produced a -0.2 dip at 17 m.
- **Crushed hardline** under a deck where weight settled over time. Localized low-Z region centered at 8 m.
- **Lightning-damaged section** at 60 m where surge had vaporized a portion of the center conductor.

In every case, TDR localized the fault to within a foot. Without TDR, you'd be looking at a 200-foot buried cable run wondering where to dig.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** For very long cables (>500 ft) the FFT-based NanoVNA TDR loses resolution because the frequency-step density isn't enough to resolve far-away features. Dedicated step-pulse TDRs (Tek 1502, Riser-Bond) maintain resolution out to thousands of feet. For amateur use under 500 ft of cable, NanoVNA is fine.
