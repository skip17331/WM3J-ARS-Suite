# AM — Amplitude Modulation

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

## What it is

**AM** is the oldest practical voice mode. A carrier of fixed frequency has its **amplitude** varied (modulated) by the audio signal. A loud part of speech makes the carrier swing high; a quiet part lets it shrink. The receiver detects the amplitude variations and recovers the audio.

AM is what AM broadcast radio uses (530–1700 kHz), what aviation uses (118–137 MHz), and what a small but loyal amateur community uses on parts of 80 m, 40 m, and 10 m.

## How it works

```
audio (voice) ─┐
               ├──→ multiplier ──→ AM modulated signal
carrier (RF) ──┘
```

Mathematically, the transmitted signal is:

```
s(t) = (1 + m · audio(t)) · sin(2π · f_carrier · t)
```

where `m` is the modulation index (0 ≤ m ≤ 1 for normal AM; m=1 is "100% modulation").

The result, in the frequency domain, is **three components**:

| Component | Power fraction (at 100% mod) | Carries information? |
|-----------|------------------------------|----------------------|
| Carrier (at `f_c`)               | 67% | No — pure tone |
| Upper sideband (USB, f_c + audio) | 16.5% | Yes |
| Lower sideband (LSB, f_c − audio) | 16.5% | Yes |

**Two-thirds of the transmitted power is in the carrier** — which contains no information. The same voice information is in BOTH sidebands (they're mirror images). This is why AM is inefficient and why SSB (which transmits only one sideband and no carrier) replaced it for most serious work.

Bandwidth: typically **6 kHz** (3 kHz audio × 2 for both sidebands). Some older amateur AM gear runs wider (~9 kHz) for "broadcast-style" full-range audio.

## Why use AM?

Three reasons amateurs still operate AM:

1. **Audio quality.** Full-bandwidth AM with a good rig and mic sounds *rich* — fuller and warmer than SSB's 3 kHz audio. AM enthusiasts care a lot about this.
2. **Heritage gear.** Pre-1960s ham gear (Collins, Hallicrafters, Hammarlund, Drake) was AM-only or AM/CW. Operating that gear is a hobby in itself.
3. **Simple receivers.** Any envelope detector can demodulate AM. Old shortwave receivers, vintage car radios, even crystal sets can hear AM amateur transmissions.

## Operating

### Where AM lives

| Band | Common AM activity windows |
|------|----------------------------|
| 80 m | 3.870–3.890 MHz (regional ragchew) |
| 40 m | 7.290 MHz (well-established AM frequency) |
| 20 m | 14.286 MHz (West Coast AM net Sunday afternoons) |
| 10 m | 29.000–29.200 MHz (10 m AM revival) |

Check [§20 Band Plans](../20-band-plans/20-00-overview.md) for current band-by-band conventions. AM activity is light most of the time; **listen first** before transmitting.

### Calling on AM

The procedure is the standard CQ flow (see [§22-04](../22-operating-practice/22-04-calling-cq.md)) with one twist: AM operators tend to be longer-winded than SSB. Expect 5–10-minute "rounds" where each operator speaks at length, then turns it over with "back to you" or "your turn."

```
"CQ AM, CQ AM, this is WM3J, Whiskey Mike Three Juliet, calling CQ AM and standing by."
```

### Rig setup

Most modern HF rigs have an `AM` mode button. Key things to check:

- **Power.** AM is rated by *carrier power* on most rigs. At 100% modulation, peak envelope power (PEP) is **4× the carrier power** — a 25 W carrier produces 100 W PEP peaks. Check your rig's manual; don't exceed the linear range.
- **Modulation depth.** Most rigs limit modulation to ~90% to prevent over-modulation distortion. Aim for clean audio, not maximum volume.
- **Audio source.** AM benefits from a good mic and audio processing (compression, EQ). Many AM operators use external audio chains with their HF rig in line mode.

### AM signal reports

Use the standard RST system but understand that on AM:

- **Carrier strength** is what the S-meter reads (the carrier is always there)
- **Modulation quality** is a separate dimension — "good modulation," "muddy audio," "over-modulated" are common comments
- **Splatter** is over-modulation generating wideband noise outside the intended 6 kHz; common AM complaint

## Common AM mistakes

- **Over-modulation.** Pushing mic gain or compression so hard that the modulation index exceeds 100% causes carrier breaking and splatter into adjacent frequencies. Listen to your own audio (a separate receiver tuned 5 kHz away should hear nothing).
- **Treating AM like SSB.** AM operators expect richer audio and longer transmissions. Quick brief calls are fine but feel out of place.
- **Operating outside the AM-friendly windows.** Calling CQ on 14.150 MHz in the middle of SSB activity will generate complaints. Stick to the AM watering holes.
- **Forgetting AM's carrier power.** Running 100 W in SSB mode might be legal, but the same setting in AM mode means 100 W carrier + 400 W PEP — possibly over your license limit at modulation peaks.

## Troubleshooting

- **Audio sounds distorted.** Check modulation depth; reduce mic gain or audio processing.
- **Receiving station says they can't hear me.** AM at the same total RF output is roughly 6 dB weaker than SSB on the receiving end (because SSB puts all power in one sideband). Expect lower signal reports than you'd get on SSB.
- **Wide bandwidth showing on a panadapter.** Normal — 6 kHz is expected. If it's wider than that, you're over-modulating.
- **Rig won't transmit in AM mode.** Some older rigs need a mode switch + power adjustment + bias setting. Check manual.

> **Advanced —** Class-E and Class-D plate-modulated AM transmitters are the gold standard for AM audio quality — they modulate the final-amplifier supply voltage directly, producing very clean modulation envelopes. Modern SSB transceivers running AM mode use low-level modulation (modulating the carrier before the linear amplifier), which is convenient but sounds different. AM purists distinguish "plate-modulated" from "low-level modulated" rigs the way audiophiles distinguish tube from solid-state.
