# FM — Frequency Modulation

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

## What it is

**FM** modulates the **frequency** of the carrier with the audio signal. A loud voice swings the carrier higher and lower in frequency; a quiet voice barely budges it. The amplitude stays constant — which gives FM its biggest advantage: noise is mostly amplitude variation, and FM receivers ignore that.

FM is the workhorse of VHF/UHF amateur radio. Every 2 m / 70 cm handheld is an FM transceiver. The vast majority of repeater traffic is FM.

## How it works

```
audio (voice) ─→ VCO (voltage-controlled oscillator) ─→ FM modulated signal
                  │
              [frequency varies with audio amplitude]
```

The carrier frequency at any instant is:

```
f(t) = f_carrier + Δf · audio(t)
```

where `Δf` is the **deviation** — how far the carrier swings from center at peak audio.

| Standard | Deviation | Audio BW | Total RF BW (Carson) | Where used |
|----------|-----------|----------|----------------------|-------------|
| **Narrow FM** | ±2.5 kHz | 3 kHz | ~12 kHz | Modern VHF/UHF amateur, public safety |
| **Wide FM** | ±5 kHz | 3 kHz | ~16 kHz | Older amateur gear, some commercial |
| Broadcast FM | ±75 kHz | 15 kHz | ~200 kHz | Commercial broadcast (88–108 MHz) |

Carson's rule for FM bandwidth: `BW ≈ 2 · (Δf + audio_BW)`.

The constant-amplitude property means **FM is amplitude-noise-immune**. AM noise (lightning crashes, ignition spark, fluorescent-light hash) doesn't change the carrier's frequency, so an FM receiver — which only cares about frequency variation — rejects it. This is why FM sounds clean above a certain signal level.

### The "capture effect"

When two FM signals arrive at the receiver, the *stronger* one suppresses the weaker one almost entirely. Unlike AM where two stations would mix into garbled audio, the stronger FM signal "captures" the receiver and you hear only it. This is why FM repeater traffic is clean even with weak interference, and why FM doesn't degrade gracefully — you either hear it cleanly or you don't hear it at all.

### The "FM threshold" or "cliff edge"

Below a certain signal-to-noise ratio (typically ~12 dB SNR at the discriminator), FM falls apart suddenly — quiet audio with a slight hiss instantly turns into 100% noise. There's no graceful degradation like SSB has. Operators describe this as the "FM cliff" or "FM brick wall."

> **Advanced —** The FM threshold comes from the discriminator's non-linear behavior in noise. Below threshold, the click rate of the FM detector becomes dominated by noise crossings of the discriminator's S-curve, generating impulse noise instead of audio. Pre-emphasis (boosting treble frequencies before transmit) and de-emphasis (cutting them on receive) extend the usable threshold by ~3 dB at the cost of slight audio coloration.

## Why use FM?

1. **Repeaters.** Almost every amateur repeater uses FM. If you want repeater contacts, you need FM. (D-STAR / DMR / Fusion are digital alternatives but the analog FM ecosystem dwarfs them in coverage and gear.)
2. **VHF/UHF simplex.** 2 m and 70 cm calling frequencies (146.520, 446.000) are FM by convention. Most VHF/UHF voice contacts happen in FM.
3. **Audio quality above threshold.** Clean, hi-fi voice with minimal noise. Better-sounding than SSB when both signals are well above their respective thresholds.
4. **Gear availability.** Cheap dual-band handhelds (Baofeng, Yaesu FT-65, Icom IC-V86, etc.) are FM. Tens of thousands of new hams start with an FM HT.
5. **Constant power output.** PA efficiency is high since the amplifier always runs at full carrier power. Battery-friendly for portable use.

## Operating

### Where FM lives

| Band | Common FM activity |
|------|--------------------|
| **10 m** | 29.500–29.700 MHz simplex; 29.500–29.700 MHz repeaters (rare but exist) |
| **6 m** | 52.525 MHz simplex calling; 51–54 MHz repeaters |
| **2 m** | 146.520 MHz calling (simplex); 145.20–148.00 MHz repeaters; 146.40–146.58 MHz simplex |
| **70 cm** | 446.000 MHz calling; 442–450 MHz repeaters |
| **1.25 m / 33 cm / 23 cm** | Smaller but active FM communities |

See [§20-02 VHF](../20-band-plans/20-02-vhf.md) and [§20-03 UHF](../20-band-plans/20-03-uhf.md) for full band plans.

### FM simplex vs. repeater

**Simplex:** both stations on the same frequency, no infrastructure. Range is line-of-sight, typically 5–30 miles with HT and 30–100 miles with a base/mobile and good antenna.

**Repeater duplex:** input frequency (where you transmit) and output frequency (where the repeater retransmits) are different. The standard offsets:

- **2 m:** ±600 kHz (e.g. output 146.94, input 146.34; "negative offset")
- **70 cm:** ±5 MHz (e.g. output 442.150, input 447.150; "positive offset")

This is technically split-frequency operation, but the rig handles it automatically when you select the repeater frequency from memory. See [§22-08](../22-operating-practice/22-08-split-frequency.md).

### CTCSS / DCS — repeater access tones

Most repeaters require a **subaudible tone** ("PL tone" in slang, formally CTCSS or DCS) on your transmit audio to access the input. Common tones: 100.0 Hz, 103.5 Hz, 127.3 Hz, 156.7 Hz. The repeater's published frequency listing includes the required tone. Without it, you key up but the repeater doesn't repeat you.

Set the tone in your rig's menu (sometimes called "Encode Tone" or "TX Tone"). If the repeater also requires a tone on receive (rare), enable "Tone Squelch" (CTCSS decode).

### Calling on FM

Simplex CQ:

```
"This is WM3J monitoring 146.520, listening for any contacts."
```

Repeater check-in:

```
"WM3J, listening."
or
"WM3J for a contact."
```

Keep it brief — FM has the capture effect, so multiple stations stepping on each other only one gets through. Repeater nets follow the net control's instructions; freelance CQs are uncommon on repeaters.

### Rig setup

Most rigs auto-select narrow FM in the amateur 2 m / 70 cm portions. Things to check:

- **Wide/narrow setting.** Modern amateurs default to **narrow FM** (12.5 kHz channel spacing). Older gear may be wide FM (25 kHz spacing) and will sound louder and brighter to a narrow-FM receiver — and may splatter into adjacent channels.
- **Power level.** FM rigs typically have low/mid/high (e.g. 0.5 / 2.5 / 5 W on HTs; 5 / 25 / 50 W on mobiles). Use minimum needed.
- **Squelch.** FM receivers have a squelch threshold below which no audio passes. Set it just above the local noise floor so weak stations open the squelch but ambient noise doesn't.
- **Tone settings.** CTCSS encode for repeaters; check the repeater's listing.

## Common FM mistakes

- **Forgetting the tone.** You key up, the repeater doesn't repeat, you assume it's broken. Check CTCSS encode is on with the right frequency.
- **Wide-FM rig on a narrow-FM repeater.** Your audio sounds blasted and you splatter into adjacent repeaters. Switch to narrow FM if your rig has the option, or replace the gear if it doesn't.
- **Holding the PTT after you stop talking.** The repeater stays keyed up — wastes its battery (if portable) and prevents others from talking. Release PTT as soon as you finish.
- **No "kerchunk".** Briefly keying the repeater to "test" without identifying ("kerchunking") violates the 10-minute identification rule and annoys other users. Identify even on test transmissions.
- **Talking through someone's QSO.** With capture effect, if you're stronger, you'll wipe them out. Listen before keying.
- **Mobile mic too far / too close.** Optimal mic distance is ~1 inch from your mouth, slightly off-axis. Too far and your audio is thin; too close and you'll over-deviate (rig may auto-limit, but voice quality suffers).

## Troubleshooting

- **Audio sounds distorted on receive.** Likely over-deviation by the transmitting station — they're talking too loud or have mic gain too high.
- **"Picket fencing" while mobile.** Multipath from terrain — signal flutters above and below the FM threshold as you drive. Slow down, find higher ground, or accept it.
- **Repeater "courtesy tone" or "drop carrier" — what do they mean?** Many repeaters emit a brief tone or pause after a transmission ends to indicate the repeater is clear. Wait for it before keying again.
- **Receive is great but nobody hears me.** Check: tone encode, transmit power, antenna SWR, microphone PTT contacts. Try a different repeater or simplex.
