# SSB — Single Sideband

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

## What it is

**SSB** is amplitude modulation with the carrier and one sideband suppressed. You transmit only one sideband — **USB** (upper) or **LSB** (lower) — and nothing else. The receiver reinserts a local carrier (BFO, beat-frequency oscillator) to demodulate the sideband back into audio.

SSB is the dominant voice mode on HF. Every HF DXpedition, every HF DX contact, almost every HF ragchew above 40 m phone, is SSB. It's also the standard for weak-signal VHF/UHF work (6 m, 2 m, 70 cm SSB).

## How it works

Start with AM. Recall the three components:

- Carrier (no info)
- Upper sideband (USB, contains the info)
- Lower sideband (LSB, also contains the info — mirror of USB)

SSB filters out the carrier and one sideband, leaving **only the other sideband**. All transmitter power goes into that single sideband — the one containing your voice.

```
AM spectrum (100% mod):
                        Carrier
                          ┃
                LSB ████  ┃  ████ USB
                    ── (3 kHz) ── (3 kHz)
                f_c-3k   f_c    f_c+3k

SSB-USB spectrum:
                         (nothing)
                          ·
                          ·  ████ USB
                          ── (3 kHz)
                         f_c    f_c+3k

SSB-LSB spectrum:
                LSB ████  ·
                    ── (3 kHz)
                f_c-3k    f_c
```

Bandwidth: **~2.4–3 kHz** (just one sideband of audio).

### Why SSB is efficient

Compared to AM at the same transmitted bandwidth:

- AM puts ~67% of power in the carrier (useless), ~17% in each sideband (one of which is redundant)
- SSB puts **100%** of power in the single useful sideband

That's roughly **9 dB better** received SNR for the same total transmitted power — about an S-unit-and-a-half improvement. This is the entire reason SSB displaced AM on HF: same gear, dramatically better range.

### USB vs LSB — which sideband?

By convention:

| Band | Sideband |
|------|----------|
| 160 m, 80 m, 40 m | **LSB** |
| 60 m and up (20, 17, 15, 12, 10, 6, 2, 70 cm) | **USB** |

This is a historical convention from when sideband filters were expensive and pre-built rigs offered one or the other. Modern rigs do both — set the right one for the band you're on.

**Listening on the wrong sideband:** the voice sounds garbled, like Donald Duck. Switch sidebands and it becomes intelligible.

## Why use SSB?

1. **HF DX.** Pretty much all HF amateur voice DX is SSB. If you want to talk to other continents on 20 m, 15 m, 10 m — you're operating SSB.
2. **Weak-signal VHF/UHF.** 6 m / 2 m / 70 cm weak-signal work (sporadic-E, tropo, EME) is SSB or CW. FM doesn't have the threshold sensitivity for weak signals.
3. **Bandwidth efficiency.** Three SSB stations fit in the space of one AM station and have better range. This is why HF voice bands are densely packed with SSB.
4. **Graceful degradation.** Unlike FM's cliff, SSB fades smoothly into noise — you can copy weaker signals with effort.

## Operating

### Where SSB lives

Most of the HF voice spectrum is SSB. Refer to band plans, but as a rough guide:

| Band | SSB phone segment |
|------|--------------------|
| 80 m | 3.700–4.000 MHz (LSB) |
| 40 m | 7.125–7.300 MHz (LSB) |
| 20 m | 14.150–14.350 MHz (USB) |
| 17 m | 18.111–18.168 MHz (USB) |
| 15 m | 21.200–21.450 MHz (USB) |
| 12 m | 24.930–24.990 MHz (USB) |
| 10 m | 28.300–29.000 MHz (USB) |
| 6 m  | 50.100–50.400 MHz (USB, weak-signal window) |

See [§20 Band Plans](../20-band-plans/20-00-overview.md) for full detail.

### Calling on SSB

Standard CQ flow (see [§22-04](../22-operating-practice/22-04-calling-cq.md)):

```
"CQ CQ CQ, this is Whiskey Mike Three Juliet, WM3J, calling CQ and standing by."
```

Phonetics improve copy in noise: "Whiskey Mike Three Juliet" not "wuh-em-three-jay."

### Pile-up and split

When working DX, the DX station often runs **split** — see [§22-08](../22-operating-practice/22-08-split-frequency.md). Almost all heavy-pileup SSB DX activity is split-frequency.

### Rig setup

- **Mode:** select USB or LSB per the band convention above. Rigs default to the right one when you tune into the phone segment.
- **Microphone gain.** Set so peaks just reach the ALC (Automatic Level Control) limit but don't pin it. Over-driving the ALC produces distortion and splatter, not more power.
- **Speech compression / DSP.** Modern rigs have audio processors that boost intelligibility on weak-signal SSB. Use at moderate levels (3–6 dB compression typical). Excess compression sounds harsh and pumping.
- **Filter bandwidth.** 2.4 kHz is the standard. 1.8 kHz is "narrow SSB" for crowded conditions; 2.7 or 3.0 kHz is "wide SSB" for high-fidelity ragchew. Match the other operator's expectations.
- **RIT (Receiver Incremental Tuning).** SSB signals are exquisitely sensitive to tuning — being 100 Hz off makes audio sound nasal. RIT lets you fine-tune the receive without changing your TX frequency.

### SSB signal reports

Standard RST applies. On SSB, common qualitative descriptors:

- "5-9" — full quieting, easy copy
- "5-7" — solid copy with noise
- "5-3" — weak, struggling to copy
- "into the noise" — barely audible
- "clean audio," "muddy," "crunchy," "pinched" — comments on audio quality

## Common SSB mistakes

- **Operating on the wrong sideband.** Garbled audio. Switch sidebands.
- **Over-driving the ALC.** Producing splatter that QRMs adjacent QSOs. Watch the ALC meter and back off mic gain.
- **Too much compression / DSP.** Sounds harsh, intelligibility drops, you don't get more contacts. Moderate settings beat aggressive ones.
- **Slow PTT release.** Hanging on the mic after you stop talking ties up the band. Release as soon as you say "over" or end your transmission.
- **Long-winded contests.** Contest exchanges are 3-4 seconds; DX exchanges are 5-15 seconds. Match the cadence of who you're working.
- **Wrong sidetone for split.** If you're trying to work DX split and your rig is set to "monitor" the transmit audio, the sidetone may confuse you about what frequency you're on. Disable monitor when running split.

## Troubleshooting

- **"You sound muffled."** Mic too far, mic gain too low, or speech compressor not engaged. Test with a local station.
- **"You're splattering ±10 kHz."** Over-driving the rig. Reduce mic gain or output power.
- **"I'm S-9 but can barely hear you."** Mismatched sidebands (you on USB, they on LSB — Donald Duck syndrome). Switch sidebands.
- **Audio sounds nasal / pinched on receive.** You're tuned slightly off. Adjust RIT (or main tuning if no RIT) until voices sound natural. ~50–100 Hz makes a noticeable difference.
- **Background hash or bird-call noises.** RFI from a switching supply, computer, LED bulb, or nearby device. Investigate ([§14 RFI](../14-rfi/14-00-overview.md)).

> **Advanced —** The performance advantage of SSB over AM (≈9 dB) is the single biggest improvement in voice mode efficiency in amateur radio history. It triples the effective range with the same transmitter power. SSB also enables modes like AM-equivalent voice via independent sideband (ISB), DSB-SC (suppressed carrier with both sidebands), and SSB-derived digital modes (PSK voice codecs, FreeDV). Modern SSB transmitters use phasing-method or filter-method sideband generation; software-defined radios use mathematical Hilbert transforms to produce SSB digitally.
