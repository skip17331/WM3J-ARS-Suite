# Estate / SK — Overview

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

In amateur radio, "**SK**" — Silent Key — means an operator who has passed away. The CW prosign that closes a contact also closes a life on the air. When that day comes for any of us, our families are left with a station full of equipment they don't understand, can't use, and don't know what to do with. Without preparation, the gear ends up at a yard sale for pennies, in a dumpster, or in the hands of an unscrupulous "dealer" who offered the widow $200 for a $5,000 station.

This chapter exists to prevent that. It walks you through preparing a document that your family can hand to a friend, a club member, or an executor, with everything they need to dispose of the station fairly — including who to call first, who to sell to, and who to absolutely avoid.

## How the chapter is organized

Two audiences live in this chapter:

| Sections | Audience | Tone |
|----------|----------|------|
| 19-00, 19-01, 19-02 | **You** — the operator preparing the document | Practical, technical, candid about market values |

## What this chapter assumes

You've already done the work in §14 (Shack Inventory). The inventory data — what you own, what it's worth, what shape it's in — is the input. This chapter is what to *do* with that data when the time comes.

If your inventory is incomplete, complete it first. The disposition document is only as useful as the inventory it sits on top of.

## Why this is worth doing

Three concrete reasons, beyond the obvious "be kind to your family":

1. **The market value of an organized, documented station is 2-3× a disorganized pile.** A buyer paying for "Mike's IC-7610 with documented service history, original receipt, current firmware, and inventory of all accessories" pays more than for "an old radio I think still works."

2. **Your friends in the hobby have first claim on the gear they've admired.** Without a document, they don't know what to ask for and your family doesn't know to call them. The first-call list (§15-04) bridges this gap.

3. **Your spouse or kids will be grateful.** A clear handoff document removes one stress at the worst possible time. The grief is going to happen regardless; the radio chaos doesn't have to.

## When to prepare this

Three rules of thumb:

- **Prepare the first version when you build the inventory.** §14 and §15 go together; finish them in the same week.
- **Update annually.** New gear, sold gear, changed values, friends' interests change.
- **Review when life changes.** Move, retirement, health event, change in family circumstances.

The document doesn't expire — but it does get stale. An annual update keeps it useful.

## What the document looks like

A complete estate handoff is roughly:

- **Cover page**: "If I'm gone — start here." Short, gentle, signed by you.
- **First-call list**: 3-10 names, what they wanted, how to reach them, what to ask.
- **Inventory printout**: from §14, sorted by category, with current values.
- **Disposition guide**: which dealers to use, how to use them, scams to avoid.
- **Vintage list**: anything in the inventory that's collectible — get an appraisal.
- **License & registration items**: club memberships to cancel, subscriptions to stop, online accounts to deactivate.
- **Final note**: 73 to the family.

Total: usually 10-30 printed pages, depending on station size. Save as a PDF in cloud storage; print one paper copy for the safe deposit box or filing cabinet.

## What this chapter is not

- **A legal document.** This is a guidance document for your family, not a will. For the legal side (who inherits, how the estate is settled), talk to an attorney.
- **A tax document.** Capital gains on equipment sales, charitable-donation valuations, etc., are tax matters. Talk to your accountant.
- **A grief counselor.** This is logistics. The emotional work is separate.
- **A substitute for talking to your family.** Have the conversation while you can. The document is the backup, not the primary plan.

## A note on tone

The family-facing sections (19-03, 19-04) are written in plain language, with the assumption that the reader is grieving and overwhelmed. Some of it will sound corny. That's intentional — corny is what works when someone is reading a list of next steps through tears.

The operator-facing sections (19-01, 19-02) are direct and practical. You're alive, organized, and planning ahead.
