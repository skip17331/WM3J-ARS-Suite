# Family Guidance

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

This section is written for the **family** — the spouse, kids, executor, or close friend left to handle the radio gear. It's a longer, gentler companion to the practical template in §15-03. If you're reading this, the operator has prepared this guide in advance because they wanted to make this part easier for you.

The operator may include this section in the printable handoff (§15-03), or refer you to it digitally. Either way, it's here to help you understand what you're dealing with and how to handle it without rushing.

---

## If you're reading this

The amateur radio operator you cared about is gone, and you're left looking at a room full of equipment you don't understand. That's hard. We're sorry.

This page is here to help, gently. There's no rush. The radio gear isn't going anywhere. You don't have to deal with any of this today, this week, or even this month. When you're ready, the steps below will walk you through it.

The most important thing to know: **you are not alone.** Amateur radio is a community. Other operators — many of whom your loved one called friends — will help you with this. The list of names and phone numbers in this document is not a chore list; it's a list of people who genuinely want to help, because helping each other through this is part of what amateur radio operators do.

---

## What was this hobby, anyway?

If you're not from a radio family, you may not have understood why someone you cared about spent so much time at this. A short explanation:

**Amateur radio is the art of long-distance communication using radio waves.** Operators talk to each other across cities, countries, and continents — often without using the internet, cell networks, or any commercial infrastructure. Just radios, antennas, and the right conditions in the atmosphere.

The hobby has been around for over 100 years. People do it for many reasons:
- The simple joy of talking to a stranger 5,000 miles away.
- The technical challenge of building stations that work well.
- The community — local clubs, contests, public service.
- Emergency communications when normal infrastructure fails (after hurricanes, earthquakes, etc.).
- The continuity with the radio operators who came before — using the same skills they did.

For your loved one, it was probably some combination of these. The equipment in front of you represents years of learning, building, fixing, and connecting with people. It mattered to them. That's why they wanted to make sure it ended up well-handled rather than dumped at a yard sale.

You don't have to understand the technical details to handle the disposition well. Just follow the document the operator left, ask questions, and trust the people on the friends list.

## How to take it slow

The grief is real and it's not optional. The radio gear is just stuff. It will wait.

A practical pace:

### Week 1-2 after the loss

You don't have to do anything radio-related yet. Take the time. Be with family. Let people bring you food.

If you must start something, the only urgent thing is **don't let anyone touch the equipment without your permission**. Lock the room, put a sign on the door, whatever it takes. You'll have time to deal with this later; you don't want anyone helping themselves now.

### Week 2-4

When you're ready: read the document the operator left. Read the cover letter. Read this page. You don't need to do anything yet — just read.

### Month 2

Call the first person on the friends list. Just talk to them. Tell them what happened. They probably already heard, and they'll be sad too. Ask them: "I have all this radio equipment to deal with eventually. Can you walk me through what's there?"

Most amateur radio operators will say yes immediately and offer to come over. They mean it.

### Month 3-4

Work through the friends list. Items that specific friends wanted, they buy. Other items they help you understand or sell.

### Month 4-6

Whatever's left, sell to the dealers in the document, donate to the local club, or set aside as keepsakes. Take the cash, deposit it, and consider that part of your life closed.

If this takes longer than 6 months, that's fine. Some families take a year. Some take two. The gear isn't going anywhere.

## The friends list

The names in the document aren't randomly chosen. The operator listed them because:

- **Some were specifically interested in specific items.** They had conversations with the operator about "if you ever sell that, let me know." Those are the people to call about those items.
- **Some are general radio friends** — people who knew the station, knew the operator, and who'll help you sort through the rest.
- **The local club** is the broader community — many members didn't know the operator personally but will help if asked.

When you call:

1. **Identify yourself**: "Hi, I'm [your name]. I'm [operator's name]'s [relationship — wife, daughter, executor]."
2. **Tell them what happened**: "I'm calling because [operator] passed away [recently/several months ago]."
3. **Tell them why you're calling**: "[Operator] left a document asking us to call you about the radio equipment. Specifically, [item if applicable], or just general help if you're willing."
4. **Listen to their response.** They'll know what to do.

Most calls end with the friend saying "I'll come by next weekend" or "Let me make some calls and get back to you." That's the system working.

## What if no one on the list answers?

It happens. People move, change phone numbers, get sick themselves. If the friends list isn't reaching anyone:

- **Try the local club** (their contact info is in the document).
- **Try the next-largest amateur radio organization**: the **American Radio Relay League (ARRL)** is the national organization. Their main number can connect you to a local section coordinator. The website is arrl.org.
- **Try a regional dealer** (HRO, R&L Electronics, etc., from the dealer list). Tell them you're handling an estate and need help. They've done this before.

The amateur radio community is connected. Even if your specific contacts aren't reachable, the broader network is. Don't give up after one or two unsuccessful calls.

## A short glossary

The document and inventory will use some terms. Here's plain-English definitions for the most common ones, in case you encounter them:

| Term | What it means |
|------|---------------|
| **Callsign** | A unique code (like "WM3J" or "K1ABC") that identifies an amateur operator. Issued by the FCC; tied to a specific person. |
| **Rig** | A radio (transmitter and receiver). Same thing. |
| **Transceiver** | A radio that both transmits and receives. Most modern radios. |
| **HF** | High Frequency — the bands where long-distance communication happens (1.8-30 MHz). The big radios. |
| **VHF / UHF** | Very High / Ultra High Frequency — shorter-distance bands (50 MHz and up). The small radios and HTs. |
| **HT** | Handheld Transceiver — the walkie-talkie-style portable radios. |
| **Antenna** | The part outside that catches and sends signals. |
| **Coax** (cable) | The thick black cable connecting the radio to the antenna. |
| **Tuner / ATU** | A box between the radio and antenna that helps the radio "match" the antenna. |
| **Amplifier / Amp** | A box that boosts the radio's transmit signal. Often valuable. |
| **Power supply / PS** | The big heavy box that runs the radio (most amateur radios need 12 V DC; the supply converts wall AC to that). |
| **Vintage / Boatanchor** | Old amateur radio equipment, often valuable to collectors. |
| **QSL card** | A postcard amateur operators exchange to confirm contacts. The operator may have a box of these. They have personal-historical value but minimal monetary value (though specific cards from rare countries can be worth $20-200). |
| **Silent Key / SK** | An amateur radio operator who has passed away. The CW (Morse code) prosign that closes a contact. The operator you cared about is now an "SK." |
| **73** | The amateur radio shorthand for "best regards." How operators close transmissions. |

## Scams to watch out for

Unfortunately, some people prey on grieving families. Specific things to be alert to:

### "I heard about your loss; I can take everything off your hands"

If someone you don't know contacts you out of the blue offering to "take everything off your hands" for a flat amount — **say no**. They're not legitimate dealers; they're predators. Real dealers don't cold-call grieving families.

### "I knew [operator]; he wanted me to have his radios"

If someone claims to have been promised specific equipment but their name isn't in the document — **be skeptical**. The operator left a document specifically because they wanted documented arrangements. Verbal promises don't override written wishes.

You can:
- Politely take their information.
- Tell them you'll check the document and call back.
- Cross-reference with the friends list.
- Call someone on the friends list to verify.

If they're legitimate, they'll wait. If they pressure you, they're not legitimate.

### "Cash deal, no paperwork, today only"

Any deal that requires speed and avoids paperwork is suspicious. Real dealers and real friends will:
- Take their time.
- Provide receipts.
- Pay via traceable methods (check, bank transfer, established payment platforms).

If someone's pushing for "cash today, no paperwork," they're hiding something.

### "I'll buy the antenna" — without inspecting

Antennas (especially towers) are complicated to remove. Removing them costs money. If someone offers cash for the antenna without inspecting it, they may not understand the removal cost — or they may plan to leave you with a damaged property. Don't accept cash for an installed antenna; arrange professional removal first, and discuss equipment value separately.

### Online buyers wanting unusual payment methods

If you sell items online (per the document's instructions) and a buyer wants you to:
- Wire money via Western Union or similar.
- Accept payment in cryptocurrency.
- Ship to a freight forwarder or PO box overseas.
- Cash a check that's larger than the agreed price (and refund the difference).

...that's a scam. Stick to PayPal Goods & Services, Venmo, Zelle for in-country sales, or established escrow services. If a buyer refuses normal payment methods, walk away.

## What if you don't want to deal with it at all?

That's a legitimate choice. Two paths:

### Sell the whole station to one dealer

The dealer list in the document includes companies that will buy a whole station for a single price. You'll get less than selling piece-by-piece (typically 30-50% of optimum value), but the transaction is fast and simple. Make one call, the dealer comes (or sends shipping instructions), the gear leaves, you get a check.

### Donate everything to the local club

The local club (or the ARRL) will gladly accept the equipment as a donation. They may use it to support new operators, run as a club station, or sell at their hamfest with proceeds going to the club. You get a charitable-donation tax receipt.

Either path is a valid choice. The operator left this document because they wanted you to have **the option** of selling at fair value, not because they required you to.

## A note from the radio community

(This section is from the broader amateur radio community to any family member reading this guide.)

When an amateur operator becomes a Silent Key, the entire community feels it. Some of us knew your loved one personally. Many of us didn't, but we shared something with them — a love of communicating through the air, of building things, of connecting across distances.

If you need help and don't know where to turn, almost any local amateur radio operator will help. Call the local club. Call the ARRL. Send an email to a dealer's customer service. We'll find someone for you.

The gear in front of you was loved. It will be loved again, by another operator who'll think of your loved one when they use it. That's the continuity of the hobby — equipment passes from one operator to another, often crossing decades and continents. The IC-7610 you sell today might be operated by someone in Wisconsin in 2035, who'll never meet your loved one but who'll be the grateful next owner of a well-maintained radio.

You're not just disposing of stuff. You're handing the next generation what came before. We're grateful you're handling it with care.
