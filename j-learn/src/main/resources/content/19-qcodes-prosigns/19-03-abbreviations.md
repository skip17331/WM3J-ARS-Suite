# Abbreviations

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

Beyond Q-codes (formal) and prosigns (procedural), amateur CW (and increasingly amateur digital modes) uses a layer of **conversational abbreviations**: short forms of common words and phrases, evolved over a century of operators trying to send "the same thing, faster." This section is the alphabetical reference.

These are not formal codes — they're conventions. Most have small variations between operators, between regions, and between generations. The list below is what you'll most commonly hear in modern amateur CW QSOs.

## The numbers

Two number-words have transcended their original CW context and now appear constantly in voice and digital modes:

### 73
- **Meaning**: "Best regards." Used to close a transmission or QSO.
- **Origin**: Phillips Code (a 19th-century railroad/telegraph shorthand) where 73 was originally "accept my compliments."
- **Usage notes**: Always "73," never "73's" (a common newcomer error). It's already plural in concept. Sent as the digits 7 and 3, not as a word.

### 88
- **Meaning**: "Love and kisses" — affectionate close-out.
- **Usage notes**: Traditionally exchanged with female operators; some couples use it routinely. Less universal than 73 — many operators don't use 88 at all.

### Other Phillips numbers (historical/rare)
- **30**: "End of report" (used in old wireline news telegraphy; vanishingly rare in amateur today).
- **22**: "Love and kisses" (alternative to 88; very rare).
- **55**: "Best success" (rare).

## The personal pronouns / addresses

These are how amateurs refer to each other on CW.

### OM — "Old man"
- The standard generic male address. "TNX OM TU" = "Thanks old man, thank you."
- "OM" doesn't mean elderly; it means "fellow male amateur."

### YL — "Young lady"
- Generic female address. "TNX YL" = "Thanks young lady."
- Like OM, age is not implied.

### XYL — "Ex-young lady"
- Specifically: the operator's wife. "MY XYL IS CALLING ME" = "My wife is calling me."
- The term is gendered and dated; modern usage varies.

### HARMONICS — Children
- "MY HARMONICS R 5 ES 7" = "My kids are 5 and 7."
- A radio-themed substitute for "kids" (a "fundamental" frequency has "harmonics" — the children of the original).

### ELMER — Mentor
- "MY ELMER WAS W1AAA" = "My mentor was W1AAA."
- An amateur who teaches another. Used widely in amateur culture beyond just CW.

### HAM — Amateur radio operator
- General. "I'M A HAM SINCE 2010."

## The conjunctions and connectors

### ES — "And"
- Substitutes for "and" in CW. "73 ES GL" = "73 and good luck."
- Sent as the two letters E and S.

### BTU — "Back to you"
- Used at the end of a transmission to hand over. "BTU OM" = "back to you old man."

### BCNU — "Be seein' you"
- Casual sign-off. "BCNU 73" = "Be seein' you, 73."

### HW? — "How copy?"
- "How are you receiving me?" Used to confirm the other station copies cleanly.

### HW CPY? — "How copy?" (longer form)
- Same as HW?

### CUL — "See you later"
- Less formal than 73; informal close.

### CUAGN — "See you again"
- Variation of CUL.

### TNX / TKS / TU — "Thanks"
- Three forms of "thanks." TU (T-U, Morse for "thank you") is the most common in CW.

### AGN — "Again"
- "PSE AGN" = "Please again." Used to ask for a repeat.

### GA — "Good afternoon"
- "GA OM" = "Good afternoon old man."

### GE — "Good evening"
- "GE OM" = "Good evening old man."

### GM — "Good morning"
- "GM OM" = "Good morning old man."

### GN — "Good night"
- "GN OM" = "Good night old man."

### GL — "Good luck"
- "GL ES 73" = "Good luck and 73."

### GUD — "Good"
- "UR SIG IS GUD" = "Your signal is good."

### FB — "Fine business"
- "FB OM" = "Excellent, old man." All-purpose approval.

### HI HI — "Laugh" / "Ha ha"
- Used for humor. "I HRD UR DOG ON THE BAND HI HI" = "I heard your dog on the band, ha ha."

### PSE — "Please"
- "PSE QRS" = "Please send slower."

## The radio / station vocabulary

### RIG — Radio / transceiver
- "MY RIG IS IC-7610" = "My radio is an Icom IC-7610."

### ANT — Antenna
- "MY ANT IS DIPOLE" = "My antenna is a dipole."

### PWR — Power (transmit)
- "PWR 100W" = "100 watts."

### RST — Readability-Strength-Tone
- The classic CW signal report. "UR RST 599" = "Your readability is 5, strength is 9, tone is 9."

### SIG — Signal
- "UR SIG IS WEAK" = "Your signal is weak."

### CONDX — Conditions
- "CONDX BAD TODAY" = "Conditions are bad today."

### WX — Weather
- "WX FB HR ES SUN" = "Weather is fine business here, sunny."

### TEMP — Temperature
- "TEMP 75 DEG" = "Temperature is 75 degrees."

### KEY / BUG / PADDLE — CW input device
- "I AM USING PADDLE" = "I'm sending with a paddle."

### NR — Number
- Used in contests with serial-number exchanges. "NR 47" = "Serial number 47."

### TFC — Traffic (NTS messages)
- "TFC FOR W1AW" = "I have traffic destined for W1AW."

## The acknowledgments

### DR — "Dear"
- Used in personal address. "DR OM" = "Dear old man."

### TKS DR OM ES 73 — Composite close-out
- A common end-of-QSO sequence: "Thanks dear old man and 73."

### R — "Roger" / "Received"
- The single-letter R is short for Roger. "R 599 IN VA" = "Roger, 599 received, in Virginia."

### RR — "Roger Roger" / "Confirmed"
- Stronger acknowledgment. "RR ES 73" = "Confirmed, and 73."

### QSL — "I confirm" (Q-code; see §19-01)

### CFM — "Confirmed"
- Alternative to QSL in some operating contexts.

## Specialized abbreviations

### CW — "Continuous wave" / Morse code mode
- "I OPERATE CW" = "I operate Morse mode."

### SSB — Single sideband

### FT8 — A digital mode (named after its protocol designers Franke and Taylor; "FT8" is part of the protocol name).

### DX — Long distance / foreign station

### DXCC — DX Century Club; the entity-counting program. "I HAVE 100 DXCC" = "I've worked 100 countries."

### POTA — Parks On The Air (operating program)

### SOTA — Summits On The Air

### IOTA — Islands On The Air

### WAS — Worked All States

### WAC — Worked All Continents

## Calling-procedure abbreviations

### CQ — Calling any station
- "CQ CQ CQ DE WM3J" = "Calling any station, this is WM3J."
- Origin: Phillips Code, where CQ meant "general call to all stations."

### DE — "From"
- "DE WM3J" = "From WM3J / This is WM3J."
- Sent as letters D-E.

### CALL — "Callsign"
- "PSE CALL AGN" = "Please send your callsign again."

### RPRT — "Report"
- "TNX FOR RPRT" = "Thanks for the signal report."

### NAME / NM — "Name"
- "NM IS MIKE" = "Name is Mike."

### QTH — "Location" (Q-code, see §19-01)
- "QTH IS VA" = "Location is Virginia."

### GRID — Maidenhead grid square
- "GRID IS FM19" = "My Maidenhead grid is FM19."

## Numerical abbreviations

In CW, especially at high speed, numbers often abbreviate:

| Standard | Abbreviated |
|----------|-------------|
| 100 | T (single dah, since `T` = `—` and represents 0 in some systems; rare) |
| 5 | E (single dit, replacing 5 in some abbreviations; rare) |
| 9 | N (single dah-dit, in fast contests; rare) |

Modern practice: send the actual digits. The above abbreviations were used in old high-speed contesting and are not common today.

## Mode-specific abbreviations

Different modes have their own conventions. A few examples:

### FT8 messages
- Built into the protocol: "73", "RR73" (Roger Roger 73), "QSL". Operators don't type these — the software sends them.

### RTTY
- Conventional CW abbreviations apply. RTTY is fast (45 baud), so abbreviations save time.

### PSK31
- Operators can type any text; abbreviations are conventional but optional. Many operators on PSK31 type out full words because keyboard speed isn't the limit.

### JS8Call
- Has built-in shortcuts for common phrases. Operators rarely type Q-codes or abbreviations; they use the menu.

## Common mistakes

- **"73's" / "best 73's"** — Reduce to "73" (already plural in concept). The "73's" form is a generational marker that some take as careless.
- **"AGN AGN" — repeated AGN** — Just send "AGN PSE" once. Repeating doesn't make the request stronger.
- **Mixing modes** — Using CW abbreviations on FM voice ("OM" on a voice repeater) is fine, but heavy-handed CW vocabulary on voice can come across as performative.
- **Inventing your own** — If you make up an abbreviation, the other op may have no idea. Stick to the conventional list above.
