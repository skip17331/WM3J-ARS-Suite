# Band Plans (HF / VHF / UHF)

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> ⚙️ **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

A **band plan** is a voluntary agreement among amateurs about which modes (CW, SSB, digital, FM, etc.) live where on each band. The FCC sets the **band edges** and a few hard rules (license-class limits, mode restrictions in narrow segments). The band plan fills in everything else.

Band plans are not law. They are convention. But violating them annoys everyone, especially when you put a fast-RTTY signal in the middle of the CW DX window or call CQ on the FM calling frequency without saying "CQ this is..."

## Why band plans exist

Different modes need different bandwidths and different signal habits. CW takes ~150 Hz; SSB takes ~3 kHz; FM takes ~16 kHz. Mixing them randomly across a band would mean every operator interferes with everyone nearby. Band plans group similar-bandwidth modes together so QRM is predictable and avoidable.

The plan is also where **DX windows**, **calling frequencies**, **beacon segments**, **CW skimmer ranges**, **FT8 watering holes**, and **special-event slots** live. Operators check the plan before transmitting in unfamiliar territory.

## The big picture — by region

Three ITU regions:

- **Region 1**: Europe, Africa, Middle East, parts of Russia
- **Region 2**: The Americas
- **Region 3**: Asia-Pacific (most of Asia, Australia)

Each region has its own band plan. They mostly agree on broad categories (CW low, SSB high) but differ on details — Region 1's 40 m extends to 7.200 MHz where Region 2's only goes to 7.300 (Region 1 lost the broadcasting overlap years before us), and FM segments differ noticeably.

This chapter focuses on the **Region 2 (US/Canada/Latin America) ARRL band plan**. If you operate from elsewhere, check the IARU plan for your region.

## HF band plans (Region 2 highlights)

**160 m (1.800–2.000 MHz)** — entirely SSB/CW, no digital subbands. CW low, SSB high. DX window 1.830–1.840.

**80 m (3.500–4.000 MHz)** — CW 3.500–3.600, digital 3.570–3.600 (FT8 at 3.573), SSB 3.600–4.000. The 3.500–3.525 segment is Extra-class CW only.

**60 m (5.330.5–5.405)** — five fixed channels, USB only, 100 W ERP max, secondary status. Strict rules.

**40 m (7.000–7.300 MHz)** — CW 7.000–7.125, digital 7.040–7.080 (FT8 at 7.074), SSB 7.125–7.300. 7.000–7.025 is Extra CW only.

**30 m (10.100–10.150 MHz)** — CW and digital only. No phone. RTTY 10.130–10.140, FT8 at 10.136. Tiny but useful.

**20 m (14.000–14.350 MHz)** — CW 14.000–14.150, digital 14.070–14.090 (FT8 at 14.074), SSB 14.150–14.350. DX window 14.195. The CW DX window is around 14.020.

**17 m (18.068–18.168 MHz)** — CW 18.068–18.110, digital 18.100–18.110 (FT8 at 18.100), SSB 18.110–18.168. WARC band — no contesting.

**15 m (21.000–21.450 MHz)** — CW 21.000–21.200, digital 21.070–21.090 (FT8 at 21.074), SSB 21.200–21.450.

**12 m (24.890–24.990 MHz)** — CW 24.890–24.930, digital 24.910–24.930 (FT8 at 24.915), SSB 24.930–24.990. WARC, no contests.

**10 m (28.000–29.700 MHz)** — CW 28.000–28.300, digital 28.070–28.150 (FT8 at 28.074), SSB 28.300–29.300, FM repeaters 29.500–29.700, AM 29.000–29.200, satellite 29.300–29.500.

**6 m (50.000–54.000 MHz)** — CW 50.000–50.100, beacons 50.060–50.080, SSB DX window 50.100–50.130 (FT8 at 50.313), FM repeaters 51.620–51.980 and 52.500–52.980, simplex calling 50.125 (SSB) and 52.525 (FM).

A useful pattern: **CW low, digital next, SSB middle, FM and special services high**. Not always exact, but a useful first approximation when you're scanning a band you don't know.

## VHF band plans

### 2 m (144–148 MHz)

| Range          | Use |
|----------------|-----|
| 144.000–144.100 | CW |
| 144.100–144.200 | EME (moonbounce) and weak-signal CW |
| 144.200–144.275 | SSB simplex (calling 144.200) |
| 144.275–144.300 | Beacons |
| 144.300–144.500 | OSCAR satellite uplinks/downlinks |
| 144.500–144.900 | FM repeater inputs |
| 144.900–145.100 | Mixed digital, packet, APRS (144.390 is the universal APRS frequency in the Americas) |
| 145.100–145.500 | FM repeater outputs |
| 145.500–145.800 | Misc / experimental |
| 145.800–146.000 | OSCAR satellite |
| 146.010–146.400 | FM repeater inputs (low half) |
| 146.400–146.580 | Simplex (146.520 is the national 2 m FM calling freq) |
| 146.610–147.390 | FM repeater outputs |
| 147.420–147.570 | Simplex |
| 147.600–147.990 | FM repeater inputs (high half) |

### 1.25 m (222–225 MHz)

Smaller and less crowded. 222.0–222.15 is CW/SSB; 222.15–223.91 is mostly FM repeaters; 223.91–225 is misc.

## UHF band plans

### 70 cm (420–450 MHz)

| Range          | Use |
|----------------|-----|
| 420–432         | ATV (amateur TV) input/output, weak-signal experimental (regional rules) |
| 432.000–432.100 | EME |
| 432.100–433.000 | SSB simplex (calling 432.100) |
| 433.000–435.000 | Various — auxiliary, experimental |
| 435.000–438.000 | Satellite |
| 438.000–445.000 | FM repeater inputs/outputs (regional plans vary widely) |
| 445.000–450.000 | Misc operational |

70 cm has the most regional variation. What's an FM repeater band in one part of the country may be ATV in another. Check your local frequency coordinator's plan.

### 33 cm (902–928 MHz)

Mostly used by digital and Part 15 ISM gear, but amateurs share it. Less coordinated. Used for some high-speed data and a handful of repeaters.

### 23 cm (1240–1300 MHz)

ATV, satellite, weak-signal narrow-band, repeaters. A good band for anyone interested in microwave technique.

## "Calling frequencies"

A calling frequency is where you call CQ when you don't know who's listening. Once you make contact, you **QSY** (move) to a clear frequency to continue.

| Band | Mode | Calling frequency |
|------|------|-------------------|
| 6 m  | SSB  | 50.125 |
| 6 m  | FM   | 52.525 |
| 2 m  | SSB  | 144.200 |
| 2 m  | FM simplex | 146.520 |
| 1.25 m FM | 223.500 |
| 70 cm SSB | 432.100 |
| 70 cm FM simplex | 446.000 |

Don't camp on a calling frequency for an extended QSO — it blocks others from finding contacts.

| Band | FT8 |
|------|-----|
| 160 m | 1.840 |
| 80 m  | 3.573 |
| 60 m  | (channelized; FT8 not common) |
| 40 m  | 7.074 |
| 30 m  | 10.136 |
| 20 m  | 14.074 |
| 17 m  | 18.100 |
| 15 m  | 21.074 |
| 12 m  | 24.915 |
| 10 m  | 28.074 |

These are dial frequencies (USB) — actual signals occupy ~3 kHz above the dial.

## Where to find authoritative plans

- **ARRL band charts** — `arrl.org/band-plan` — the US authoritative source.
- **IARU regional plans** — `iaru-r1.org`, `iaru-r2.org`, `iaru-r3.org`.
- **ARRL color band chart** — printable PDF, every shack should have one taped to the wall.
- **J-Learn floating bandplan overlay** (when shipped) — pulls the same data into a panel inside J-Digi/J-Map/J-Bridge.

> ⚙️ **Advanced —** Band plans are technically only enforced through peer pressure, but the FCC has occasionally cited operators for "willful interference" when they planted high-bandwidth digital signals in the CW DX window or transmitted on frequencies clearly designated for satellite uplinks. The legal threshold is hazy; the social threshold is sharp. If your mode is going to take 10 kHz, find a 10 kHz hole, don't carve one in the middle of the CW segment.
