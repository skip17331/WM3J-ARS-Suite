# Shack Ergonomics

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

§27-04 laid out the shack as a *system* — where the gear goes and how it's wired. This section is the **human** side of the same desk: posture, reach, lighting, and the things that decide whether a six-hour contest leaves you tired-but-happy or wrecked. Ergonomics is the cheapest performance upgrade in the station — a $300 chair improves your score more than a $300 antenna accessory, because the operator is the part that fatigues.

## Chair and desk — get the geometry right

- **The chair is the most important purchase in the shack.** Adjustable height, real lumbar support, armrests at a height that lets your shoulders relax. Buy this before the second radio.
- **Desk height** so your forearms are roughly **horizontal (elbows ~90°)** when operating the key, keyboard, and mic. Too-high desks cause shoulder and wrist strain over a long session.
- A **footrest** helps if the chair-to-floor geometry doesn't quite work for your height.

## Screen, controls, and reach zones

- **Monitor:** top of the screen **at or just below eye level**, about an arm's length away, on a monitor arm so you're not craning your neck. Two screens? Put the primary one straight ahead, not angled off to one side you'll twist toward all night.
- **Primary reach zone:** the controls you touch constantly — VFO knob, PTT/footswitch, paddle, and the logging keyboard — belong directly in front of you within easy reach. Secondary gear (tuner, amp, SWR meter) can go to the sides or above.
- **Key/paddle and mic:** position the paddle so your **forearm rests** on the desk (a floating arm cramps within minutes), and put the mic on a **boom or stand at mouth height** so you're not hunching toward it. A boom mic + footswitch frees both hands for logging and tuning.
- **Headphones** beat a speaker for both fatigue and weak-signal copy, and they keep the XYL happy.

## Lighting

- **Task light** on the desk for reading the log, notes, and rig markings — but angled so it doesn't glare off screens.
- A **bias light** (a dim light *behind* the monitor) markedly cuts eye strain during long night sessions by reducing the contrast between a bright screen and a dark room.
- Everything **dimmable** — the shack you contest in at 0300 wants far less light than the one you answer email in at noon.

## Surviving the long session

> **Advanced —** Repetitive-strain injury is the real occupational hazard of contesting and heavy CW operating. The mitigations: a **neutral wrist** at the paddle and keyboard (no sustained bending), a **voice keyer / CW memory keyer** so you're not manually sending the same CQ a thousand times, scheduled **micro-breaks** (stand and stretch every hour — even a contest can spare 60 seconds an hour), hydration within reach, and airflow/temperature you can stand for the duration. Operators who plan 24- and 48-hour contests treat sleep, food, and posture as part of the station design, not an afterthought.

## Accessibility

The same adjustments that prevent fatigue make the station usable for more operators: voice keyers and memory keyers reduce repetitive strain, screen scaling and high-contrast logging software help low-vision operators, and large/adaptive switches can replace fiddly buttons. Designing for comfort and designing for accessibility are the same project.
