# Pile-up Etiquette

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

A **pile-up** is what happens when many stations call one station at once — a rare DX entity, a special-event operator, a famous callsign, a popular DXpedition. Done well, a pile-up moves dozens of contacts per minute. Done poorly, it's chaos that wastes everyone's time and burns out the operator on the rare end.

The rules are unwritten but well-established. Newcomers earn the rare contacts; veterans earn the next-rare contact and the one after that.

## Two kinds of pile-up

| Type | What it is | Operating tactics |
|------|------------|--------------------|
| **Pile-up to a CQ** | Many stations responding to one CQ | Brief calls, one or two callsigns, listen carefully |
| **Pile-up around a DX station** | The DX station is "running" and managing many callers | Specific calling discipline; honor split-frequency operating |

Most rare-DX activity is the second type. The DX station calls CQ once, then "runs" — making one contact after another with a steady cadence, often using **split-frequency** operation to manage the pileup.

## Split-frequency operation

Heavy DX pile-ups almost always run **split** — the DX transmits on one frequency and listens on a different range (typically 5–15 kHz higher) so they can hear individual callers instead of a wall of noise. The rest of this section assumes you know what split is and how to operate it. If you don't, read [§22-08 — Split-Frequency Operation](22-08-split-frequency.md) first; it covers the technique, rig setup, the announce shorthand ("5-up", "QSX", etc.), and the full call-and-response protocol.

The pile-up "sins" below are about *discipline* once you understand split. The cardinal sin — transmitting on the DX's TX frequency — gets you publicly named as a "lid" (incompetent operator) and is universally hated.

## How to call in a pile-up

### Before you call

- **Listen.** Hear the DX call CQ; understand their split (where they're listening). Know their cadence (slow, fast, calm, rushed). Listen for at least 30 seconds before transmitting.
- **Pick your TX frequency.** The DX is listening across (e.g.) 5 kHz. Don't pick the exact center — that's where most callers go. Pick a quieter spot.
- **Know your callsign perfectly.** "WM3J" — say it crisp, clear, phonetic, ready. No hesitation.

### When to call

- **Wait for the DX to finish a contact** (you'll hear "QRZ" or "5NN, QRZ"). That's your window.
- **Call once, briefly.** "Whiskey One Alpha Bravo Charlie" — that's it. Don't add "calling DX" or any padding.
- **Listen.** Don't keep calling if you're not picked.

### When you're picked

The DX will say your callsign + signal report:

```
DX: "W1ABC 5-9, QRZ"
```

Your response is brief:

```
You: "5-9 thanks 73 W1ABC"
```

That's the complete contact. No conversation, no "great signal," no "weather here is fine." Pick up your call from the log, move on.

### When you're not picked

- **Stop calling.** If the DX picks someone else, your transmission is now QRM to that contact. Stop, wait for "QRZ," try again.
- **Don't repeat your call indefinitely.** If you've called 5-6 times and not been picked, move to a different TX frequency in the listen range, or come back later. The DX may be processing partial copy of your call and will name you on the next "QRZ."
- **Don't say "thanks" or "73" if not picked.** That just adds noise to the band.

## Pile-up sins (and what to do instead)

### "Cop"-pers

A "copper" calls on the DX's TX frequency to attract attention. Universally hated. Never do this. The DX will name them ("the lid on my TX frequency, off frequency please") and they get publicly mocked.

**Right thing**: call on the DX's listen frequency.

### "DX-only" or "Up only" callers

Some operators yell "DX only" or "WAS only" if they want to discourage callers in their own country. This is rude. Just don't call if you don't qualify (you're inside the DX's country, you don't need this entity for an award you're chasing, etc.).

**Right thing**: don't call if you don't apply.

### Repeat callers

Calling the DX 8-10 times in a row gets you noticed (negatively). The DX has heard you; they're working someone else; let them.

**Right thing**: call once or twice, listen, give other operators a turn, try again later.

### Late callers

Calling 30 seconds after the "QRZ" is when most pile-up activity has died down. You're calling into a dead pause. The DX has moved to a new caller already.

**Right thing**: call within 5-10 seconds of "QRZ" or wait for the next one.

### Callers on TX freq

Operators who claim "I can't tell which is the TX freq, so I'll just call on whatever I'm tuned to." Wrong. The DX has clearly stated their split (e.g., "5-up").

**Right thing**: read the band; understand the split; transmit on the listen frequency.

## DX cluster spotting

The DX cluster (a network of DXer-run nodes) shares spotting information:

```
WM3J  W3LPL    14205.0  ZL2  9PM  599  Tnx Rich
                          ^^^   ^^^  ^^^
                    DX    SP    Note (signal report, time)
```

If you see a spot, it tells you a rare DX is active on that frequency. Tune there, listen, and call appropriately. The cluster shortens "find the DX" from minutes to seconds.

Spotting yourself is generally frowned upon — let other operators decide if your contact is worth spotting. Self-spots are visible and embarrassing.

## Special-case pile-ups

### Contest pile-ups

Contest operators run very fast — 30-60 contacts per minute is common. The exchange is minimal:

```
You:  "WM3J 59 003"
DX:   "WM3J 59 047 QRZ"
```

Your callsign + signal + serial number is the entire transmission. No "thanks," no "73." Brief means more contacts.

### DXpedition pile-ups

Major DXpeditions run for days, taking thousands of contacts. The operator on the rare end is **exhausted** by hour 10. Bad behavior is more frustrating to them, not less.

Best practice: be quick, accurate, polite. They're trying to log everyone; help them.

### Special-event station pile-ups

For special events (1×1 callsigns, anniversary stations), the cadence is moderate. The exchange may include the event name:

```
You: "K3R Field Day 5-9 WM3J"
K3R: "WM3J Field Day 5-9 73"
```

Read the operator's announcement to know what exchange they want.

## When the DX is QRT (closing for now)

The DX may signal end-of-session:

```
"WM3J 73 QRT for now, will be back in 30 minutes"
"QSY to 17 m"
"Going QRT for the night"
```

Stop calling. The DX is closing. Try again later.

## Common pile-up mistakes

- **Calling on the DX's TX frequency.** The cardinal sin. Always call on the listen frequency.
- **Long calls.** "Whiskey One Alpha Bravo Charlie portable from Massachusetts" wastes the DX's time. Just "W1ABC."
- **Adding "thanks" or commentary.** "5-9 thanks for the contact" — you've now used double the bandwidth of the actual exchange. The DX moves on faster if you stop sooner.
- **Repeat-calling.** Calling 6+ times when not picked is noise. Pause.
- **Picking the same TX freq as everyone else.** If 50 callers are on 14.210, calling on 14.210 makes you indistinguishable. Try 14.211 or 14.212 — 1-2 kHz of separation can make you stand out.
- **Calling without listening to who's QRP.** If the operator says "QRP only" or "stations west of the Mississippi," and you're not, don't call.
- **Spotting yourself.** Embarrassing. Let others spot you.
- **Showing off in the log.** A pile-up isn't a forum to demonstrate you can copy at 30 WPM. Get the contact and move on.

## What good pile-up operators do

A few habits to develop:

- **Listen for 30 seconds before transmitting** to understand the situation.
- **Time your call** — wait for "QRZ," call once, listen.
- **Pick a quiet TX frequency** in the listen range — often 1-2 kHz away from where everyone else is.
- **Be brief.** "WM3J" is enough.
- **Be patient.** Rare DX takes patience. You'll get the contact eventually.
- **Use a small rig and modest antenna** sometimes — to know what it's like without the advantages of high power and big antennas. Trains better operating habits.

> **Advanced —** Pile-up management is a skill the DX operator works at. Variants include "single-band, single-mode," "split with auto-rotate" (the DX listens at slowly varying frequency to disperse callers), "ZL listen" (only New Zealand calls), "EU listen" (only European calls), and "QRZ DX" (DX only, but the DX operator confirms each call). Veteran DX operators can sustain 60+ contacts per minute for hours; the operator on the rare end is the one being trained over and over again. The "lid" subculture in amateur radio is half-joking, half-genuine — operators who repeatedly violate pile-up etiquette get named publicly on social media and DX cluster comments.
