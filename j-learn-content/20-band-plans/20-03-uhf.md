# UHF

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

The amateur UHF allocations cover **70 cm, 33 cm, 23 cm**, and several higher bands (13 cm, 9 cm, 5 cm, 3 cm, etc.) that fewer amateurs use. This section focuses on the three most-used UHF bands; the higher bands are summarized at the end.

All Technicians and higher have full privileges across UHF amateur bands; no license-class sub-band restrictions apply.

## US UHF amateur allocations

| Band | Frequency range | Bandwidth |
|------|-----------------|-----------|
| 70 cm | 420.0–450.0 MHz | 30 MHz |
| 33 cm | 902.0–928.0 MHz | 26 MHz |
| 23 cm | 1240.0–1300.0 MHz | 60 MHz |
| 13 cm | 2300.0–2310.0; 2390.0–2450.0 MHz | varies |
| 9 cm | 3300.0–3500.0 MHz | 200 MHz (note: shared with primary users) |
| 5 cm | 5650.0–5925.0 MHz | 275 MHz |
| 3 cm | 10000.0–10500.0 MHz | 500 MHz |
| Higher | 24 GHz, 47 GHz, 76 GHz, 122 GHz, 134 GHz, 241 GHz | various |

A note on **secondary status**: most amateur UHF allocations are *secondary* to primary users (radar, military, scientific, satellite). Amateurs must not cause interference to primary users and must accept any interference from primary users.

## 70 cm (420.0–450.0 MHz)

By far the most-used UHF band. Repeaters are densely deployed; FM voice dominates; weak-signal SSB and CW occupy the lower edge. Many amateurs operate dual-band rigs that cover 2 m and 70 cm equally.

### 70 cm repeater pairs

US 70 cm repeaters conventionally use a **5 MHz offset**:

- **Output 442.000-445.000**: input is 5 MHz lower (437.000-440.000) — but practical inputs are usually in the 444-446 segment.
- More common pattern: **Output 442.X / Input 447.X** (positive 5 MHz) or **Output 444.X / Input 449.X**.

Conventions vary considerably by region; consult the local ARRL frequency coordinator's database for accurate repeater pair info.

### 70 cm calling frequencies

- **446.000 FM** — national FM simplex calling.
- **432.100 CW** — weak-signal CW calling.
- **432.200 SSB (USB)** — weak-signal SSB calling.
- **432.300 (de facto)** — FT8 on 70 cm during openings.

## 33 cm (902.0–928.0 MHz)

A wide band (26 MHz), but **shared with several primary users** (Wi-Fi, ISM, point-to-point links, location services). Amateur use is light in most regions and varies dramatically by area.

### Voluntary 33 cm mode plan

| Frequency | Convention |
|-----------|-----------|
| 902.000-902.500 | Weak signal CW / SSB |
| 902.100 | SSB calling (USB) |
| 902.500-903.000 | Beacons |
| 903.000-903.400 | OSCAR uplinks |
| 904.000-904.500 | Repeater outputs |
| 905.000-908.000 | Mixed FM |
| 909.000-915.000 | Repeater inputs / ATV |
| 919.000-922.000 | Mixed / experimental |
| 922.000-928.000 | Mixed; ATV; ISM-shared region |

The 902-928 MHz band is heavily shared with cordless phones, ISM equipment, public safety, and Wi-Fi. Amateur use here often involves narrow-band weak-signal experimentation rather than heavy repeater deployment.

### 33 cm calling frequency

- **903.100 FM** — sometimes used for simplex; varies by region.
- **902.100 SSB (USB)** — weak-signal calling.

## 23 cm (1240.0–1300.0 MHz)

A 60 MHz amateur allocation. The **only amateur microwave band heavily used for FM repeaters** in some regions. Also home to ATV, weak-signal experimentation, and increasingly digital voice modes.

### 23 cm calling frequency

- **1294.500 FM** — sometimes used for FM simplex; regional.
- **1296.100 SSB (USB)** — weak-signal calling, EME, terrestrial DX.

## 23 cm and higher: weak-signal / EME / experimental

The bands above 33 cm are increasingly the domain of:

- **EME (moonbounce)** — high-gain dish antennas, kilowatt-class power, narrow signals bouncing off the moon. 1296 MHz, 2304 MHz, 5760 MHz, and 10368 MHz are the commonly-used EME frequencies.
- **Microwave terrestrial DX** — line-of-sight contacts at hundreds of miles via tropospheric ducting or rain scatter.
- **Amateur TV (ATV)** — analog and digital video transmission.
- **Mountaintop / contest VHF/UHF** — operators set up high-altitude stations to maximize line-of-sight range.

The full mode plans for these bands are typically organized by **regional VHF/UHF coordinators** rather than by ARRL or FCC.

## Higher bands (3 cm and up)

Increasingly the domain of microwave specialists. Coordination is local; check with regional coordinators.

| Band | Notes |
|------|-------|
| 3 cm (10 GHz) | Active EME and terrestrial DX community; 10368.100 SSB calling |
| 5 cm (5.7 GHz) | Some amateur TV and weak-signal use |
| 24 GHz | Specialty equipment; rare |
| 47, 76, 122, 134, 241 GHz | Very few active stations; one-off DX records |

## ATV (Amateur Television)

Multiple bands include ATV segments:

- **70 cm**: 420.0-426.0 (channel A1); 438.0-450.0 (channel A2 with offset).
- **33 cm**: parts of 902-928.
- **23 cm**: 1240-1246, 1252-1258.
- **13 cm**: 2410-2450.

ATV is now mostly digital (DVB-T, DATV) replacing analog NTSC/PAL ATV. Bandwidth requirements (~6 MHz for analog, narrower for digital) require these bandwidth-rich allocations.

## Common mistakes on UHF

- **Treating 70 cm repeaters as having the same offset as 2 m.** They use 5 MHz, not 600 kHz. Verify your rig's offset before keying up.
- **Operating 70 cm SSB in the FM repeater segment.** The repeaters are heavily used; SSB sidebands will be heard as interference.
- **Mistaking 33 cm shared frequencies for amateur-exclusive.** 902-928 includes ISM, cordless phones, Wi-Fi backhaul, and amateur use — all simultaneously. Coordinate locally.
- **Thinking microwave is always weak-signal.** It's often weak-signal *because* of low gear/availability, not low *demand*. The 1296 EME community runs serious power.
- **Skipping the regional coordinator's input.** Repeater pair conventions vary by region; "standard" 70 cm offsets in one region may be reversed in another.
