# WSPR — Weak Signal Propagation Reporter

> **Advanced callout convention:** sections or paragraphs intended for Extra-class / engineering depth are marked with a blockquote starting `> **Advanced —**`. Simple-mode renderers can hide these; advanced-mode renderers show them inline.

WSPR (pronounced "whisper") is not a contact mode — you don't work anyone. It's a **propagation-measurement beacon**: stations transmit a tiny, structured signal at low power, every receiver that decodes it uploads a spot to **wsprnet.org**, and the result is a live, global map of which paths are open right now and how strong. It answers two questions better than any other mode: *"is the band open to there?"* and *"is this antenna actually working?"* — and it does so with signals far too weak to hear. Created by Joe Taylor (K1JT) in 2008, it's part of the same WSJT family as FT8 (§03-01).

## What it sends

A WSPR transmission carries just three things, packed into 50 bits:

- your **callsign**
- your **4-character Maidenhead grid** (e.g. FM19)
- your **transmit power in dBm**

That's it — no message, no exchange. The point is the *envelope*: who heard you, where, and at what signal-to-noise.

## How it works — slow and narrow buys sensitivity

| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Modulation | 4-FSK, continuous-phase |
| Tone spacing | 1.4648 Hz |
| Symbol rate | 1.4648 baud (≈0.683 s/symbol) |
| Occupied bandwidth | **~6 Hz** |
| Transmission length | 162 symbols ≈ **110.6 s**, starting 1 s into an even UTC minute |
| Decode threshold | about **−28 dB SNR** (in a 2500 Hz reference bandwidth) |
| T/R period | 2 minutes |

The trick is the same as the other WSJT modes, pushed to an extreme: an extraordinarily slow, narrow signal concentrates all its energy into a 6 Hz sliver over nearly two minutes, giving enormous processing gain. WSPR routinely decodes signals **20+ dB below the noise floor** — paths you'd never know were open by ear.

> **Advanced —** The 50 information bits are protected by a long convolutional code (constraint length 32, rate ½) and interleaved, yielding the 162 transmitted symbols. Power is entered in **dBm** and only values of the form n×10 + {0, 3, 7} are legal — so 30 dBm = 1 W, 37 dBm = 5 W, 23 dBm = 200 mW, 0 dBm = 1 mW. Because the message is fixed-format, the decoder knows exactly what structure to look for, which is part of how it pulls callsign+grid+power out of −28 dB. Like all WSJT modes it needs an accurate clock (±1–2 s); the §03-00 clock table lists WSPR among the time-disciplined modes.

## It's a QRP / QRPp game

WSPR is built for low power. Typical beacons run **1–5 W**, and the sport is seeing how far you get per watt — many operators run **milliwatts** and still cross oceans. Because the report includes your power, wsprnet.org effectively ranks "miles per watt" for you. See §11 for the power-budget side of that.

## Frequencies

WSPR lives in tiny 200 Hz windows at coordinated dial frequencies (USB), with the audio tones around 1400–1600 Hz. A few of the common HF dials:

| Band | Dial (USB) |
|------|-----------|
| 40 m | 7.038600 MHz |
| 30 m | 10.138700 MHz |
| 20 m | 14.095600 MHz |
| 17 m | 18.104600 MHz |
| 15 m | 21.094600 MHz |
| 10 m | 28.124600 MHz |

Set the dial, and the whole 6 Hz signal sits inside the audio window.

## Software and hardware

- **WSJT-X** — has a WSPR mode built in; the usual starting point with a rig + sound card.
- **Dedicated beacons** — QRP Labs **U3S** / **QDX**, **ZachTek** transmitters, and Raspberry-Pi beacons (WsprryPi / rpitx) run a WSPR beacon 24/7 on milliwatts without tying up your main rig.
- **wsprnet.org** — the database and map: upload your decodes, then query where *you* are being heard. WSPR Rocks and other front-ends give nicer visualizations.

## What it's good for

- **Antenna A/B testing** — run WSPR on antenna A for an hour, then antenna B, and compare the reception reports objectively instead of guessing.
- **Propagation monitoring** — watch bands open and close in real time across the world; a far better "is 10 m open?" check than calling CQ into a dead band.
- **QRP/QRPp experimentation** — how little power makes the path.
- **24/7 beacons** — a milliwatt beacon mapping your station's reach around the clock.
- **Pico-balloon telemetry** — high-altitude balloons use WSPR's extended/telemetry encoding to report position globally on a few milliwatts (the "U4B"-style channels).

## What it is not

- **Not a QSO mode** — there's no contact, no ragchew, no DXCC credit. If you want weak-signal *contacts*, that's FT8 (§03-01).
- **Not anonymous** — you're publicly broadcasting your callsign, grid, and power to a global database.

## Common mistakes

- **Clock not synced** — no decodes; discipline the PC clock to NTP/GPS.
- **Wrong dial frequency** — you're transmitting outside the 200 Hz window and nobody coordinates with you.
- **Transmitting too often** — set a low TX fraction (e.g., 20%) so you *listen* most of the time; a band full of beacons that never receive is useless.
- **Dirty signal / ALC** — keep ALC at zero and drive clean; even a constant-envelope mode splatters the narrow window if overdriven.
- **Wrong power figure** — the report is only useful if the dBm you entered matches what you actually transmit.
